The Read

Read all sixteen. Here's what's actually in the pile, told straight.

What you've got: Two Wikipedia articles (Hawara, Hall of Records), a Herodotus fetch that returned nothing but a Perseus library catalog page, a serious Current Archaeology-style feature, a Historic Mysteries piece, an Ancient Origins-style "Part 3" writeup, a comments-heavy page from the Mataha Foundation's own site, a 2026 dig-announcement blog, two fact-checks of an unrelated 2025 Giza scandal, and five videos. On paper that's sixteen independent looks at the question. It isn't. The entire "it's still down there" case runs through one small, interlocking crew — Louis De Cordier (who ran the 2008 Mataha survey), William Brown, Carmen Boulter, Timothy Akers, and Trevor Grassi's Archaeological Rescue Foundation — who fund, cite, and interview one another across at least seven of these sixteen items: the Joe Rogan clip, the Why Files episode, both of Johanna's videos, the Mataha Foundation's page, the AncientHistoryX dig report, and the Archaeological Rescue Foundation's own flagship "Hawara Project" document, which says outright that "at least one member of our current extended team was present at every one" of the four scanning missions. Seven sources telling one story once each is not seven confirmations.

The story, assembled: The best move in the pile is an inversion, and it's the thing to lead with. Six classical authors — Herodotus, Strabo, Diodorus Siculus, Pliny, Manetho, Pomponius Mela — describe a building at Hawara bigger and more astonishing than the pyramids, three thousand chambers, half open to visitors, half sealed and guarded, a stone roof "like the walls," no timber anywhere. In 1888 Petrie found a giant stone platform and concluded he was standing on the labyrinth's demolished foundation — orthodoxy ever since. De Cordier's whole case is one question: what if that slab wasn't the floor, but the roof the ancients described? His 2008 Mataha survey, run with Egypt's own NRIAG and Ghent University, scanned beneath it and reported a granite grid at 8–12 meters, ten football fields wide. Then, by every network account, Zahi Hawass's council banned release, citing national security; De Cordier waited two years, self-published in 2010, and was blacklisted. After that the claims escalate with each retelling — Boulter's 2014 satellite work adds a flooded level over a dry one; Akers's 2015 Merlin Burrows scan adds four levels, a dome the size of Hagia Sophia, two intact wooden boats, and a 40-meter freestanding object with a "metallic" radar signature unlike anything he'd seen. Akers and Boulter are both now dead, and Akers's data sat under a ten-year NDA until 2025 — every wildest claim routes through witnesses who can no longer be asked follow-up questions. A separate strand grafts on the Hall of Records: Edgar Cayce's 1930s prophecy of an Atlantean archive under the Sphinx's paw, kept alive by the 1990s Schoch/West/Hancock erosion-and-Orion argument, gets explicitly relocated by one video from Giza to Hawara — "maybe the Hall of Records isn't in Giza, maybe it's in the labyrinth." The story lands, for now, on May 2026: Egypt's own Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities opens an official dig at Hawara, and the network reports a megalithic wall already uncovered.

The real fights: Three genuine disagreements. First, gone versus still-there: Wikipedia's Hawara entry and the best scholarly piece in the pile both treat the labyrinth as a demolished mortuary-temple complex, and the scholarly piece — citing Egyptologist Eric Uphill's reconstruction and Alan Lloyd's argument that only Herodotus and Strabo actually visited — dissolves the "one colossal building" into an ordinary pyramid complex the Greeks over-schematized. It never mentions the 2008 scans at all. One mid-tier source bridges the lanes dishonestly, claiming the radar found rooms "packed with magnificent hieroglyphs and murals" — radar cannot see murals. Second, suppression versus disclosure politics: the cover-up story keeps tripping on its own logic — Egypt granted the permits, Egypt's own institute ran the scan, and in 2026 Egypt opened an official dig, which a real cover-up would never allow. The Rogan guest supplies the better theory himself: "I kind of don't really blame him... I think this was a political decision" — Egypt controls the announcement, not the truth. Third, the Khafre problem: two fact-checks dismantle a March 2025 claim of a vast underground city at Giza from Malanga, Biondi, and Ciccolo — no peer review, physically implausible radar depths, a viral cross-section image that tests as AI-generated, and on-record rejections from Hawass, former minister al-Damaty, and Egyptologist Abdel-Basir. The Hawara network welds itself to that exact team anyway: the flagship document names Biondi as Hawara's next scanner once his Giza work wraps. The debunkers never mention Hawara; their ammunition transfers regardless, because the believers loaded it themselves.

What's honestly missing: The actual scan data — the NRIAG report and the Mataha whitepaper are linked inside these sources but never quoted at length, so most of the pile describes evidence rather than showing it. Hawass's own account of the Mataha episode never appears. No independent expert speaks about Hawara specifically — the debunking sources address Giza's Khafre claims only. And nothing here comes from outside the network on the 2026 dig itself; the only report of it is the network's own blog, waiting, as it says, for the next update.

The Players

Dr Zahi HawassHead of Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities and later Minister of Antiquities, the official who signs off on these projects and the one who shut several of them down

The Mataha Expedition ran in conjunction with the Supreme Council of Antiquities while Hawass headed it, alongside Egypt's National Research Institute for subsurface studies. Shortly after the team gave its Ghent lecture, the Secretary General of that same council halted all communication of the findings, supposedly under Egyptian national security sanctions, and De Cordier says he and his team were threatened directly. The same shape repeats elsewhere. He called Schoch and West's Sphinx research American hallucinations and banned them, then a year later, in 1996, led a new ten million dollar project digging around the Sphinx, and was filmed entering a tunnel saying even Indiana Jones would never dream to be there. Two years in he reversed, closed the project, and called claims of anything under the Sphinx nonsense. When Andrew Collins reported a crack leading to a chamber system near the tomb of the birds in April 2008, Hawass denied the caves existed and banned him, then was later filmed entering those caves. He dismissed the Scan Pyramids findings when they were independently announced, then stood at the podium for the official announcement of the same findings. On the documented holes in the Sphinx, one on the head sealed with concrete, one under a block leading to a tunnel, and one on the back he was filmed looking into, his answer is that treasure hunters made them and nothing was found, with no photo or video offered. He debated Graham Hancock on 15 April 2015, on the condition that he present second after seeing Hancock's slides. Hancock's account, and it is only Hancock's account, is that Hawass turned on a slide referencing Robert Bauval, shouted, made demeaning comments about Bauval, threatened to walk out if Bauval was named, and left before negotiations finished.

Carmen BoulterResearcher who organised the first satellite-based scan of the site and read its flooded and dry layers; died in 2022

Boulter put together the first satellite scan, run by GeoScan Systems, with help from Louis De Cordier, Andrew Barker, Klaus Dona and Michael Donnelan, which places her inside the same small circle as the Mataha people rather than off to one side. Her team built a virtual model from the satellite data showing two separated levels, a flooded level above shown in blue and a dry level below shown in red, and she did the analysis reading the blue as water and the red as clear. Before that she made the docu-series The Pyramid Code, so a lot of the public framing of this material traces back through her. Mark Carlotto later compiled two papers on satellite SAR sensing that carry her work forward. She died in 2022, so nothing new can be asked of her and her claims now reach a reader only through De Cordier, Carlotto, or her own films.

Edgar CayceAmerican psychic who coined the term Hall of Records and gave the story its Atlantis layer; died in 1945

Cayce was born in 1877 and was known as the Sleeping Prophet because he spoke his readings while asleep. Over 43 years he gave more than 14,000 documented readings, including medical diagnoses for patients he had never met, and he is credited with calling the 1929 crash, the deaths of two American presidents, the rise and fall of Hitler, the collapse of the Soviet Union, and a Black 44th president. He also predicted his own death for 1 January 1945 and said he would be buried four days later, then died of a stroke three days later. In readings given between 1923 and 1944 he described a Hall of Records under the Giza plateau holding 32 tablets that documented Atlantis, written in both Atlantean and Egyptian script so they would work like a Rosetta Stone, and in 1932 he said it sat under the front paws of the Sphinx and would be found in the 1990s, perfectly preserved. He put the Sphinx at roughly 12,000 years old rather than 4,500, and said Atlantean survivors carried their knowledge to Egypt, the Yucatan and the Bahamas. He was the first person to use the phrase Hall of Records and was a forerunner of the New Age movement. He has been dead since 1945, so every Cayce claim in this story is a quotation from the readings and nothing can be put back to him.

Ghent University of BelgiumBelgian university that co-led the Mataha Expedition with Egypt's NRIAG and hosted the October 2008 lecture where the findings were first shown in public

Ghent is the academic half of the Mataha Expedition. The survey was led by Ghent together with the National Research Institute of Astronomy and Geophysics in Egypt, and the results were presented at a public lecture on the Ghent campus on Tuesday 28 October 2008. The lecture was free and the Belgian press attended, but turnout was poor, which matters because that quiet room is where the data first went public. Shortly afterwards the Secretary General of Egypt's Supreme Council of Antiquities halted all communication of the findings, supposedly under national security sanctions, so the Ghent lecture ends up being the last open moment before the shutdown. The material filed under this name also gathers the other institutions circling the same sites, with no stated Ghent link: Cairo University, the Grand Egyptian Museum, two unnamed British universities, a group called NAG led by Dr Abbas, Florida State with Schor at Giza from 1996, Higashi Nippon International University and Tohoku University working with NRIAG on the Khafre anomaly reported by Smithsonian Magazine in May 2024, Filippo Biondi of Strathclyde and Corrado Malanga of Pisa on the Khafre Project, Lawrence Conyers of the University of Denver calling the underground city a huge exaggeration, the University of North Carolina Wilmington team that found a buried Nile branch beside the pyramids in 2024, and Sean Rigby of Nottingham, who works on ancient glass and holds MAs in Ancient History and Ancient Religions from Wales Trinity Saint David.

Louis De CordierBelgian artist and entrepreneur who funded and ran the Mataha Expedition at Hawara, and the source of the suppression story

De Cordier spent two years getting a permit, then partnered with Egypt's National Research Institute of Astronomy and Geophysics to survey Hawara with ground penetrating radar from early 2008. The radar reached through about 50 feet of sand and sediment and returned a grid of granite walls 40 feet down covering roughly 750,000 square feet, about ten football fields. He also wrote the report that ties the whole case together, running from Herodotus through Flinders Petrie to the expedition's own scan results, and he is the one who challenged Petrie's reading of the big stone slab, arguing it could be the roof of the labyrinth with the complex sitting underneath. On-site work finished around 2008 and the team prepared to release. Instead they were told to stop all discussion of the project, and De Cordier says they were threatened with national security sanctions from Egypt, with the communication ban coming from Hawass as Secretary General of the Supreme Council of Antiquities. He waited two years for an official release that never came, published the data himself in June 2010, was blacklisted, and moved to southern Spain to farm almonds. His blacklisting became a cautionary tale inside this world: Akers and his team sat on their findings for a decade because of it, and released in 2025 without an excavation permit. He also helped Carmen Boulter with the first satellite scan, so he links the radar work and the satellite work.

Merlin BurrowsUK scanning company whose 2015 satellite imagery is one of the datasets stacked under the site

Merlin Burrows is described as a legitimate company using technology developed for UK defence and military work. Its 2015 satellite pass is said to show layered structures, a central atrium, and a symbolic boundary described as an Omega moat or Shen ring. Newer data attributed to the company reportedly picked up a metallic object underground. It sits in a stack of remote sensing evidence alongside Mark Carlotto's 2023 work and the 2008 Mataha VLF-EM survey, so it is one of several independent-looking passes over the same ground rather than a standalone find. Along with Geoscan, the company claims it can scan kilometres deep and has done that kind of work for oil and mineral extraction firms, but says it needs landowner permission signatures before it can scan Giza, which is the practical wall in front of most of this. The name appears in the material with different spellings, as Merlin Burrow and Merlin Burroughs.

William BrownCivil engineer and founding director of the Archaeological Rescue Foundation, who led the first ground penetrating radar scans at Hawara in 2007 to 2009

Brown and his wife Lucyna Lobos Brown ran the first GPR work at Hawara, a collaboration between the University of Wroclaw in Poland and the University of Cairo, after earlier scans of the plateau with Dr Abbas Mohamed Abbas in 2006. Those scans showed the pyramid structure running much deeper under the sand than anyone had it, revealing its real underground corner, and picked up an entrance shaft below the pyramid leading down to the labyrinth. As an engineer he also did the first proper look at the water problem, drilling boreholes at chosen points to test the groundwater, and wrote the first report proposing ways to deal with it, which he presented at a 2009 conference at the University of Cairo. He now supports Lewis's Hawara project quietly, along with Trevor Grassi, without either of them putting their names to it publicly, partly because they talk about Atlantis and that is unwelcome with Egyptian authorities. Two cautions for a reader. This is the same man as the Bill Brown card. And the material filed under his name also carries facts about Sir William Matthew Flinders Petrie, a completely different person, the archaeologist who dug at Hawara in 1888 and 1889 looking for the tomb of Amenemhat III, found papyri from the 1st and 2nd centuries AD, and remains the only person to have mapped the pyramid's interior chambers before flooding sealed them off.

Abbas Mohamed AbbasEgyptian geophysicist at NRIAG who led the Mataha Expedition scans and is the Egyptian institutional link across most of the Hawara work

Abbas led the scanning side of the Mataha Expedition for Egypt's National Research Institute of Astronomy and Geophysics, working in partnership with Ghent University in Belgium. Before that he ran GPR scans of the Hawara plateau with Bill Brown in 2006, so he predates the Mataha work on that ground and connects the two survey efforts. He also heads a group called NAG, listed among the institutions on the project alongside Cairo University, the Grand Egyptian Museum and two British universities. He is still active and publishing: he and his team put out a further report in 2024 with updated water level figures for Hawara, which matters because water is the thing standing between anyone and the chambers. Of the main names here he is the one working from inside the Egyptian scientific system rather than from outside it.

Alan LloydCommentator on the classical sources who argues most ancient accounts of the labyrinth were secondhand

Lloyd's role in this story is to thin out the ancient record. He argues that of all the classical writers, only Herodotus and Strabo actually went to the site, and that the rest were working from hearsay, which cuts the number of independent eyewitnesses down to two. He also flags a translation problem in Herodotus, the phrase aulae katastegoi, usually rendered as roofed courts, which Lloyd reads as a contradiction because aula normally meant an open courtyard. The material gives nothing else about him, no dates, no affiliation, and no indication of where he published, so his standing has to be taken on the argument alone.

Ancient EgyptHeading covering the classical record of the labyrinth and the modern Egyptian permission system that controls access to it

Ancient Egypt, Heading covering the classical record of the labyrinth and the modern Egyptian permission system that controls access to it. Two separate things sit under this name. The first is the paper trail. Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus, Pliny the Elder, Strabo and Pomponius Mela all wrote about the labyrinth across a span running from about 500 BC to the first century AD, placing it near Lake Moeris and near Crocodilopolis, also called ancient Arsinoe. Herodotus, writing in the 5th century BC, said a priest took him underground and showed him something he struggled to accept as man-made. The Inventory Stela, found by a French archaeologist in the 1850s, records Khufu repairing the Sphinx after a lightning strike, which does not fit a timeline in which Khufu died before the Sphinx was built. The second thing is control. Any dig in Egypt runs through the Council of Antiquities, now the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities, which decides what gets released and when. De Cordier and his team were threatened with national security sanctions. In 1935 authorities put a gag order on stories about underground tunnels, and the reporting stopped, including Wright's claim of a 5,000 year old underground subway system running beneath the causeway between the second pyramid and the Sphinx.

Bill BrownShort form of William Brown's name, used in the interview material for the same civil engineer who ran the 2006 to 2009 Hawara scans

Bill and William Brown are one man, and a reader should treat the two cards as one person. Under this name the material adds the working detail. He scanned the Hawara plateau with Dr Abbas Mohamed Abbas in 2006. In 2008 he led a Polish team that drilled boreholes around the pyramid to test water levels and took soil samples. Also in 2008, while running GPR along the outer edges, the scan strayed into the pyramid itself and picked up an entrance shaft down to the labyrinth, something that has been known since 2009 without being acted on. He wrote the first report on how to deal with the groundwater and presented it at a University of Cairo conference in 2009. His earlier Hawara project put 50 students through master's degrees, and a similar student programme is planned again. He and Trevor Grassi now back Lewis's Hawara work from behind the scenes, deliberately keeping their names off it because the Atlantis talk around the project does not sit well with Egyptian authorities, so his current involvement is real but unattributed.

City of CrocodilesThe ancient city the classical writers used as the landmark for the labyrinth, and therefore the anchor for where to look

The City of Crocodiles, or Crocodilopolis, stood on the western bank of the Nile in the Heptanomis, between the river and Lake Moeris, southwest of Memphis. Its Pharaonic name was Shedyt, and under Ptolemy II it was renamed to honour his sister-wife Arsinoe, which is why the ancient sources sometimes call it Arsinoe. Today it is Medinet el-Fayum. Herodotus, in Histories Book II section 148, put the labyrinth a little way above Lake Moeris and nearly opposite this city, with a pyramid standing at its corner, and that sentence is the reason anyone digs at Hawara rather than anywhere else. The priests told him the underground levels held burial vaults of sacred crocodiles, mummified with bronze teeth and jewelled eyes, which is where the city's name and the labyrinth's contents meet. The material filed here also drifts into modern claims of underground cities: Biondi and Malanga's Khafre Project announcement on 23 March 2025 of a vast underground city under Giza said to run more than 6,500 feet, which spread on X, Instagram and TikTok. Johanna's contributions point elsewhere again, to an underground city in Turkey as a precedent for building refuge below ground, and to Sarbug as an old name for a city in Iran, which she raises as a hint that more than one labyrinth may have existed.

Diodorus SiculusGreek historian of the 1st century BC, one of six ancient writers who describe the labyrinth, and probably not an eyewitness

Diodorus wrote about the labyrinth in his History, Book I, sections 61 and 66, more than a hundred years after Strabo. He said the building had a roof made from a single stone, and that a king called Mendes or Mares built it as his tomb. He also claimed Daedalus built the Cretan labyrinth for King Minos after seeing the Egyptian one, and that unlike the Cretan version, which vanished, the Egyptian labyrinth still stood perfect in his own day. That Crete connection is the weakest thing he says, and the material states flatly that there is no historicity to it. He sits in the group of six ancient sources with Herodotus, Strabo, the 3rd century BC priest Manetho, Pliny the Elder and Pomponius Mela, and by Alan Lloyd's reckoning he falls on the hearsay side of that group, since only Herodotus and Strabo appear to have been there.

Giza PlateauThe second site the same cast keeps working on, where the scanning, the denials and the Atlantis claims all repeat

Giza is where this story goes when it is not at Hawara, and the overlap is the point. Herodotus described a pyramid 240 feet high near the labyrinth, carved with animal figures, with an underground passage said to run to the pyramids at Memphis, which people today read as Giza. Cayce placed his Hall of Records under the Giza plateau in readings given between 1923 and 1944. Hamilton M. Wright ran a decade-long clearing project on the plateau that ended in 1935. The Scan Pyramids team used muon and cosmic ray detection there and found voids inside the Great Pyramid, one large and one smaller near the chevron blocks at the main entrance. Andrew Collins found a crack leading into a chamber system near the tomb of the birds in April 2008, reported it, was denied and banned by Hawass, who was later filmed entering the same caves. Hawass also lost his temper over a slide about Robert Bauval, who tied Orion's Belt to the three Giza pyramids and Leo to the Sphinx and dated the Sphinx to 10,500 BC. Ben of the YouTube channel Uncharted X made a video on a tunnel system under the plateau, which Johanna watched, and those tunnels are said to run about 8 miles to Saqqara, connecting the Sphinx and the pyramid to it. One physical contrast worth keeping straight: the Hawara pyramid is mud brick with little left above ground, while Giza is hard stone. Johanna also sells merchandise using her own ancient history tattoo, which shows the Giza pyramids, the constellation Leo and a blue lotus.

SECTION 4

The Files

File: Ancient accounts of the Labyrinth

established

The Labyrinth of Egypt was written about extensively by ancient authors. The guest on the Joe Rogan Experience lists them as Herodotus, Diodorus Siculus, Pliny the Elder, Strabo and Pomponius Mela, and says these writers span roughly 500 BC up to the first century AD. Sean Rigby's article puts the count at six ancient authors who mention the Labyrinth: Herodotus, Strabo, the 3rd century BC priest Manetho, the Greek historian Diodorus Siculus of the 1st century BC, the Roman writer Pliny the Elder of about AD 23 to 79, and the Roman geographer Pomponius Mela, writing around AD 43. The Historic Mysteries article gives almost the same roster and makes the point that Herodotus was not the first historian to describe ancient Egypt's labyrinth. It names Manetho Aegyptiaca in the 3rd century BC, Diodorus Siculus in the 1st century BC, Strabo from 64 BC to 19 AD, Pliny from 23 to 79 AD, and Pomponius Mela around 43 AD, and says at least two of them claimed to have visited the labyrinth themselves. Rigby's framing is that the Egyptian 'Labyrinth' is a largely vanished monument that greatly impressed Classical writers.

The sources do not agree on when Herodotus was there. Rigby dates him c.484 to 425 BC. The compilation on the Archaeological Rescue Foundation page, excerpted from Louis De Cordier's Mataha Expedition report, dates him ca. 484 to 430 BC. The Why Files episode puts him at Hawara around 450 BC. Johanna, on her own channel, says he went there at "like 150 AD I think or BC, one of them," and she flags her own uncertainty as she says it. Her date does not match any of the others. What all of them agree on is the claim itself. Writing in his Histories in the fifth century BC, Herodotus said the labyrinth surpassed the pyramids in grandeur. The quote the Rogan guest reads out runs: "For this I saw myself and I found it greater than words can say. For if one should put together and reckon up all the buildings and all of the great works produced by the Hellenes, the Greeks, they would prove to be inferior in labor and expense to this labyrinth." The fuller version in the De Cordier compilation adds that the temple at Ephesos and the temple at Samos are works worthy of note, that the pyramids too were greater than words can say, and that the labyrinth surpasses even the pyramids. Rigby quotes the same passage in a different translation: "I have seen it myself, and indeed words cannot describe it. Though the pyramids beggar description and each one of them is a match for many great monuments built by Greeks, this maze surpasses even the pyramids." The Historic Mysteries version has Herodotus saying in the second book of his Histories that this was a work he had never seen before, a work that defied description, and that even the Greeks' vast monuments and the pyramids paled by comparison.

On the layout, Herodotus reported twelve roofed courts with doors facing each other, six facing north and six facing south, all inside one outer wall, and double sets of chambers totalling three thousand, fifteen hundred above ground and fifteen hundred underground. The Archaeological Rescue Foundation compilation gives the same numbers and describes the twelve courts as having gates facing one another. Herodotus wrote that the roof over the whole thing was made of stone, like the walls, which is the line De Cordier picks out from Histories Book II, 148. He also wrote that at the corner where the labyrinth ends there stood a pyramid of forty fathoms, with large figures carved on it, and a way made underground to it. The video about the tunnels under Giza renders that same passage as a pyramid 240 feet in height with carved figures of animals on it and an underground passage by which it can be entered, and adds that Herodotus was told these underground chambers connect the pyramid with the pyramids at Memphis. That video then identifies the Memphis he meant as the Giza Plateau today. That identification is the video's own gloss, not something in the quoted passage.

Herodotus was clear about what he did and did not see. The upper set of chambers he walked through himself. The chambers underground he heard about only, because the Egyptians who had charge of them were not willing on any account to show them, saying that here were the sepulchres of the kings who had first built the labyrinth and of the sacred crocodiles. The Why Files episode covers this and adds detail the Herodotus quotation does not carry: it says the priests told him the lower levels held the burial vaults of sacred crocodiles, mummified, wrapped in linen with bronze teeth and eyes made of jewels. The bronze teeth and jewelled eyes are the episode's dressing. The underlying quotation, which the episode itself reads out a moment later, says only sepulchres of the kings and of the sacred crocodiles. The Historic Mysteries piece takes a different line again and says Herodotus claimed to have counted three thousand rooms, with walls covered in hieroglyphs and paintings, making up a funerary complex hidden beneath a pyramid. Counting all three thousand sits awkwardly with Herodotus's own statement that half of them were described to him rather than shown.

On location, Herodotus placed the Labyrinth a little way beyond Lake Moeris and near the place called the City of Crocodiles, which Rigby identifies as Arsinoë, capital of the Fayum, and he wrote that near the corner where the Labyrinth ends stands a pyramid. The Rogan guest gives the same two markers, Lake Moeris and a city called Crocodilopolis, also known as ancient Arsinoe. Strabo placed it differently. He wrote that the Labyrinth had adjoining to it the tomb of the king who constructed it, and that he came across it after proceeding beyond the first entrance of the canal joining the Nile to Lake Moeris about 30 or 40 stadia, where there is a table-shaped plain with a village and a large palace.

Strabo, dated c.64 BC to AD 24 by Rigby and ca. 64 BC to 19 CE in the De Cordier compilation, called the Labyrinth "a work equal to the Pyramids," a building containing peristyle courts equal in number to the nomes of Egypt, with hidden chambers so twisting that no one could enter or leave without a guide. De Cordier cites him as Geography, Book 17, I, 3 and 37, the passage in which Strabo wrote that the roofs of each chamber were made of single stones. The Why Files episode, which puts him about four centuries after Herodotus, says he found the labyrinth still there and still in use. His description is of a large palace composed of as many sub-palaces, the aulae, as there were nomes, arranged contiguously in one line, with the entrances into the courts opposite a long wall, and with long winding covered ways in front of the entrances so that no stranger could find his way in or out without a guide. The roofs of each chamber were made of single stones, and the covered ways were roofed the same way with single slabs of extraordinary size, with no timber or any other material included. Looking down into the aulae from the roof, they lie in a row each supported by twenty-seven pillars, each consisting of a single stone, and the walls are of stones no smaller. De Cordier's excerpt of Strabo also records that the total number of nomes was equal to the number of courts in the Labyrinth, and that these are fewer than 30. The Why Files reduces the roof detail to a roof made of a single stone with no wood or other material involved, then dramatizes it as millions of tons of rock floating over their heads. The stone-slab roofing is in Strabo. The floating rock is the episode talking.

Diodorus Siculus, writing in the 1st century BC, said in his History, Book I, 61 and 66, that the building had a roof made of a single stone. He wrote that when the king died and the government was recovered by Egyptians, they appointed a native king Mendes, whom some call Mares, and that this king built what is called the Labyrinth as a tomb. He added that it is said by some that Daedalus crossed over to Egypt and, in wonder at the skill shown in the building, built for Minos, King of Crete, a labyrinth like the Egyptian one, in which the Minotaur was kept, and that unlike the Cretan Labyrinth, which has completely disappeared, the Egyptian Labyrinth remained absolutely perfect in its entire construction down to his own time. Wikipedia's Hawara page states flatly that there is no historicity to the assertion of Diodorus Siculus that this was the model for the labyrinth of Crete that the Greeks imagined housed the Minotaur. The Why Files episode says Diodorus Siculus and Pliny the Elder wrote about the labyrinth over a hundred years after Strabo. That ordering does not fit the 1st century BC date every other source here gives Diodorus, and the sources are left disagreeing about it.

Pliny the Elder said the Cretan labyrinth of Daedalus was only a hundredth-part copy of the Egyptian one. Rigby quotes him from Naturalis Historia 36.13: "that Daedalus used this as the model for the Labyrinth which he built in Crete is beyond doubt, but it is equally clear that he imitated only the hundredth part of it which contains twisting paths and passages, all impossible to negotiate." The Why Files gives the same claim in its own wording, that the famous Greek labyrinth was just a cheap copy. Manetho, by contrast, referred to the Labyrinth merely as a 'tomb'. Alan Lloyd has suggested that only Herodotus and Strabo actually visited the site, and that the other ancient accounts were second-hand, based on hearsay.

The ancient accounts do not reconcile. Rigby's article says plainly that the accounts of Herodotus and Strabo, the two authors usually treated as the more reliable sources, differ significantly on the fundamental number of courts or chambers. Flinders Petrie suggested the total number of nomes in Egypt was 42, with 22 in the Fayum alone, and that figure does not match either man's numbers closely. Some of the uncertainty comes from translation. Where the text is rendered 'roofed courts', Herodotus used a term transliterated as aulae katastegoi, and Alan Lloyd regards that as a contradiction in terms, since aula normally meant an open courtyard before a house while katastegoi means roofed-over. One explanation offered is that the roofed part was a peripheral colonnade surrounding an otherwise open space, and that Herodotus was struggling to describe unfamiliar Egyptian architecture in terms his Greek readers could follow. Attempts to reconcile the two men have also suggested that Herodotus only got to see the outer part of the complex while Strabo was allowed further in, perhaps because the outer parts had been destroyed in the meantime. Rigby's conclusion is that the over-exaggerated ancient descriptions likely arose from Graeco-Roman unfamiliarity with Egyptian architecture and from sensationalized attempts to describe it in terms a Graeco-Roman readership would find impressive, and that until much more systematic and wide-ranging modern excavation is carried out, a reliably authentic reconstruction of the Labyrinth is not possible.

Historic Mysteries takes the opposite posture. It calls Herodotus one of our most useful sources on the ancient world, says his writings are usually reliable, and notes he is often the only source we have for certain ancient events and places. It records that he claimed to have personally visited a labyrinth in Egypt during his travels. It also says many historians had considered his description of the labyrinth to be a rare instance of unreliability from him, and that recent discoveries have thrown doubt on that interpretation. The same article notes that Amenemhat III built the pyramid in the 19th century BC, so that when Herodotus visited the site it was already more than a thousand years old.

Wikipedia's Hawara entry gives the sober version of the same story. The huge mortuary temple that originally stood adjacent to the pyramid is believed to have formed the basis of the complex of buildings with galleries and courtyards called the "Labyrinth" by Herodotus, and this building was also mentioned by Strabo and Diodorus Siculus. The Mataha Expedition page describes Hawara as the site believed to house the legendary Egyptian Labyrinth, a colossal structure described by Herodotus, Strabo and Pliny as surpassing even the pyramids in grandeur. The article recounting that expedition opens by noting that more than 2,000 years ago the ancient Greek historians Herodotus and Strabo recorded their visits to the legendary labyrinth of Egypt.

The Why Files episode compresses all of this into a single sweep. For a thousand years, historians traveled to Egypt to see the famous labyrinth of Hawara, and they said it was so massive it made the pyramids look small. The episode puts its size at about 3 million square feet, 70 acres of rooms and corridors stacked on multiple levels. Historians wrote about it for 600 years, across different eras, different languages and different cultures, and they all described the same thing. Herodotus, the episode says, reported that the construction was beyond human ability and that it surpassed even the pyramids and all the buildings of the Greeks combined. The upper floor alone was massive, and around the maze of rooms were 12 enormous chambers called courts, each representing one of Egypt's regional governments. There were 3,000 chambers in total, above and below ground, and the underground ones were not shown to him. The Egyptian priests allowed visitors on the upper floors but kept the chambers below ground off limits, saying those rooms belonged to the kings who built the labyrinth. And the episode makes a point of the one detail every historian agrees on: nobody was allowed to enter the lower chambers, and for 2,000 years nobody did.

Johanna's video gives the version she found in the historical record. Around the Hawara pyramid, she says, is a huge underground labyrinth with two floors and up to 3,000 chambers, and it was the place where they stored the antiquities, the secrets and all of the pre-Egyptian history. She reads Herodotus at length, then reads a second passage she also attributes to him: "when one had entered the sacred enclosure one found a temple surrounded by columns 40 to each side, and this building had a roof made of a single stone carved with panels and richly adorned with excellent paintings. It contained memorials of the homeland of each of the kings as well as the temples and the sacrifices carried out in it, all skillfully worked in paintings of the greatest beauty." That same passage appears word for word in the De Cordier compilation, where it is attributed to Diodorus Siculus, History Book I, 61 and 66, not to Herodotus. The two sources disagree on whose words those are. What catches Johanna is the phrase "memorials of the homeland." She wonders aloud whether 'homeland' means Atlantis, and grounds the guess in Egyptian oral tradition recorded at the Temple of Edfu, which she says holds that the Egyptians were survivors of a deluge from an island that disappeared in a day and a night, after which they traveled to Egypt, recolonized and restarted Egyptian civilization. The region is called the Faiyum. The sources do not agree on how far Hawara is from Cairo, and it is worth putting the numbers side by side rather than picking one. The Why Files episode says Hawara sits at the edge of the Faiyum Oasis about 50 miles south of Cairo, near a crumbling black pyramid, and that today it looks like a pile of rubble. Johanna's video, whose main topic is the Hawara site, puts it about 100 miles south of Cairo. The Wikipedia entry gives the flat description. It is the site of a pyramid built by King Amenemhat III of the 12th dynasty, between the 19th and 18th centuries BC. Amenemhat III was the last powerful ruler of the 12th Dynasty. The pyramid at Hawara is believed to post-date the Black Pyramid he built at Dahshur, and is believed to have been his final resting place. The intact pyramid tomb of Neferuptah, his daughter, was found at Hawara about 2 kilometers south of the king's pyramid. The Historic Mysteries article says the Pyramid of Hawara was constructed by Amenemhat III, the last great ruler of Egypt's 12th dynasty at the height of the unified Middle Kingdom period.

On how the pyramid was built and what became of it, the Wikipedia entry says that in common with Middle Kingdom pyramids built after Amenemhat II, it was constructed of mudbrick around a core of limestone passages and burial chambers, and faced with limestone. Most of that limestone facing was later pillaged for use in other buildings, a fate common to almost all of Egypt's pyramids. Today the pyramid is little more than an eroded, vaguely pyramidal mountain of mud brick, and of the once magnificent mortuary temple precinct there is little left beyond the foundation bed of compacted sand and chips and shards of limestone. Johanna's video describes the same thing from the ground. What she stresses is that the interior and the underground portions are megalithic and remain intact. You can no longer go inside, she says, because a rising water table has flooded it, the Nile having once flowed right up near the pyramid.

The Wikipedia entry also carries the demolition history. The demolition of the Labyrinth may date in part to the reign of Ptolemy II, under whom the Pharaonic city of Shedyt, Greek Crocodilopolis, modern Medinet el-Fayum, was renamed to honour his sister-wife Arsinoë. A massive Ptolemaic building program at Arsinoë has been suggested as the ultimate destination of Middle Kingdom limestone columns and blocks removed from Hawara and now lost. The entry adds that Pharaoh Sobekneferu of the 12th Dynasty also built at the Hawara complex, and that her name meant "most beautiful of Sobek", the sacred crocodile. The Historic Mysteries article puts it more loosely, saying little remains of the original labyrinth structure today, that much of it was demolished by later Egyptian dynasties who pillaged it for dressed stone and other building materials, and that what remains now is little more than a ghostly outline on the landscape. That article also offers a reading of what the building was for. The labyrinth appears to have originally been a huge mortuary temple, and the Egyptians would have used its many rooms for funerary rites and cult worship of the dead pharaoh associated with the nearby royal tomb. The Why Files episode compresses all of this into one line, that the labyrinth was eventually looted and quarried for stone, which led archaeologists to claim nothing remained but rubble and sand.

Karl Lepsius comes first in the survey history, and the sources describe his conclusion in two different ways. The Joe Rogan conversation says Lepsius surveyed the site in the 1700s, found the ruins of a Roman town built on the surface, and concluded there was nothing significant there. Sean Rigby's article says the Prussian Egyptologist Karl Richard Lepsius gave the most thorough nineteenth-century account, that he observed a great platform on which the pyramid and the buildings south of it had once stood, and that he found Roman mud-brick remains which he mistakenly identified as the Labyrinth. So one source has him dismissing the site and the other has him claiming to have found the very thing. The dates do not line up either. The Rogan conversation puts him in the 1700s, while the Wikipedia entry says the labyrinth was first located in modern times by Karl Lepsius in 1843.

Flinders Petrie is the centre of this part of the story. The Joe Rogan conversation says he excavated at Hawara in the late 1800s and early 1900s and dug down seven or eight meters, where he found a massive stone slab of beton or plaster roughly a thousand feet long. He traced its edges and concluded he was standing on the foundation of the labyrinth, and that the building above it had been quarried away and destroyed. That conversation is explicit about the consequence. Since Petrie's conclusion, orthodox Egyptology and sources like Wikipedia have stated that the labyrinth was destroyed or quarried away and no longer exists.

The Why Files episode tells Petrie's dig as a set piece, with a comic sidekick interrupting the narration and lines written for dramatic effect, so the performance is the episode's own. The underlying facts it is dramatising sit in Petrie's own account and in the Wikipedia entry. Petrie discovered that the pyramid was made of mud brick held together by loose sand, which made it unstable, and that it was rigged with traps, including sliding trap doors, false passages, and stones set to drop on intruders. After tunnelling south he found a door with steps leading underground, though the steps were submerged in salty, filthy water because centuries of canal irrigation had raised the water table. He reached a central chamber carved from a single block of yellow quartzite, roofed with three quartzite slabs each weighing 45 tons, and found it empty, because tomb robbers had already looted it. Then, studying the field of rubble beside the pyramid, he found the remains of a building covering 70 acres, and the location matched ancient maps and the dimensions matched ancient descriptions, so he concluded he was standing on the foundation of the lost labyrinth of Hawara. The episode says he believed the labyrinth was destroyed and went back to England disappointed, not realising that what he had found was only the roof of a structure that extended much further underground. That last step, the roof reading, is the episode's claim rather than Petrie's.

The Wikipedia entry lays out the trap system in detail, and its account of how Petrie found it differs from the Why Files version. From the pyramid entrance a sloping passageway with steps runs down to a small room and then a further short horizontal passage. Hidden in the roof of that horizontal passage was a concealed sliding trapdoor weighing 20 tons. If a robber found and opened it, he would meet either an empty passage closed by wooden doors or a parallel passage carefully filled with mud and stone blocking, which was designed to make him waste time clearing it. A second 20-ton trapdoor sat in the roof of the empty passage and gave onto a second empty passage at a right angle to the first. A third 20-ton trapdoor gave onto a passage at a right angle to its predecessor, so the passages circled the interior of the pyramid. The final passage ended in a large area of mud and stone blocking that presumably concealed the burial chamber, but this was a blind, filling a wide but shallow alcove. Two blind shafts in the floor, carefully filled with cut stone blocks, wasted the robbers' time further. The real entrance to the burial chamber lay between the blind shafts and opposite the alcove. Despite all of it, Petrie found that none of the trapdoors had been slid into place and that the wooden doors were open. That is a plain difference from the Why Files line about Petrie disarming the traps one by one.

The burial chamber itself was made from a single quartzite monolith lowered into a larger chamber lined with limestone. Petrie estimated the monolithic slab at 110 tons. A course of brick was placed on the chamber to raise the ceiling, and then the chamber was covered with 3 quartzite slabs estimated at 45 tons each. Above the burial chamber were 2 relieving chambers, topped with 50-ton limestone slabs forming a pointed roof, and over that an enormous arch of brick 3 feet thick was built to support the core of the pyramid. When it was found there were two sarcophagi in the quartzite monolith burial chamber, with room for at least two more. When Petrie excavated the chamber he found a bead of lapis lazuli for inlaying, burned bone fragments, burned wood, and charcoal, which suggests tomb robbers had burned the wooden coffins and the mummy of the pharaoh.

Johanna's video describes the same interior in different mineral terms. She says that in the 1800s Petrie excavated the Hawara pyramid when the water table was lower, and that he found a large yellow-stone calcite box, alabaster, precision-made, similar to boxes found in the Saqqara tunnel system. She adds that huge yellow calcite slabs line the interior of the pyramid, a feature she says she has not seen in other Egyptian pyramids, and that calcite or alabaster has conductive properties. She does not take the science further than the assertion. Note that where she says calcite and alabaster, the Why Files episode and the Wikipedia entry both call the chamber stone quartzite. Johanna also covers the slab. In the 1800s, she says, Petrie found a huge stone slab he believed was the foundation of the labyrinth structure, and concluded it had been destroyed over time.

North of the pyramid Petrie found a vast necropolis containing 146 portraits on coffins dating to the Roman period, the "Fayum" mummy portraits, considered among the very few surviving examples of painted portraits from classical antiquity. Among his discoveries was a great papyrus scroll containing parts of books 1 and 2 of the Iliad, known as the "Hawara Homer", now held at the Bodleian Library in Oxford. The Wikipedia entry says Petrie's first excavation at Hawara can be retraced in four overlapping phases. Phase 1 was the excavation of the Pyramid of Amenemhat III itself, which is distinct from the Black Pyramid. Phase 2 was a survey of the "Site of the Labyrinth", cut short after only limited discoveries, after which the excavation's resources were redirected to the pyramid and the surrounding tombs. Phase 3 began after a painted portrait was discovered in the "Scattered Tombs" north of the pyramid, and Petrie was quick to see the worth of it. "If we can get one or two a week," he wrote in his journal, "we shall be well repaid." He was also interested in potential eugenic interpretations of the artefacts, and wrote a letter to his fellow eugenicist Francis Galton discussing his findings. After increasing pressure from competition posed by other antiquities dealers, Petrie finished excavating both the pyramid and the northern cemetery. Phase 4 entailed the excavation of the "very promising" "Crocodile Tomb-Chapels" north-east of the pyramid.

The measurements of the slab differ between sources, though two of them agree. The two-part Mataha feature says Petrie discovered an enormous artificial stone plateau measuring 304m by 244m, that is 304 meters by 244 meters,, that he interpreted it as the foundation of the labyrinth, and that he concluded the labyrinth itself must have been destroyed in antiquity, leaving only the stone base. Against that, the Rogan conversation gives only a rough thousand feet long, and the Why Files episode gives 70 acres.

Rigby's article carries the fullest account of what Petrie actually mapped on the labyrinth site. Petrie found the rectangle divided roughly in half from east to west by a wall running along its middle, and he unearthed the foundations of a great retaining wall at the southern end. Along the western boundary he found the ruined traces of two stone walls separated by a space. In the southern area, close to the extended middle axis of the pyramid, he discovered a large quartzite sandstone door jamb, which suggested a great entrance to the Labyrinth building. Column fragments suggested the roof had been barrel-vaulted, and red granite columns were found only north of the central dividing wall. The presence of stone building fragments suggested the site was a temple rather than a palace or an administrative centre, since those would typically have been built in mud brick. The site was identified as built by Amenemhat III because of fragments of red granite architraves bearing his cartouche and the name of his daughter Sobekneferu, found within the pyramid enclosure. South of the pyramid, just east of the central axis, two red granite shrines were found, with remnants of another to the west, and each of them contained two sculpted figures of Amenemhat III. Further statue fragments east and south-east of the shrines included white limestone statues of the goddess Hathor, the god Sobek, a goddess wearing a headdress of four palms or feathers, and Amenemhat III with two goddesses holding fish. North-east of the pyramid a great cemetery area was found containing shaft-tombs and mastabas, which Petrie called "the area of crocodile burials", and which may correspond to the pteron, or "wing", mentioned by Pliny.

Petrie also tried to reconstruct the building. He developed a reconstruction of the Labyrinth combining his own observations, those of Lepsius, and the ancient textual descriptions. He accepted that the chamber layout he reconstructed bore no resemblance to the typical ground-plan of Egyptian temples, and he proposed that this was exactly what caused the confusion of ancient visitors and, from there, the association with the Cretan Labyrinth.

Eric Parrington Uphill, who lived from 1929 to 2018, proposed an alternative reconstruction. He worked by comparison, setting Hawara against the other known Twelfth Dynasty pyramid complexes of Amenemhat I, Senusret I, Amenemhat II, Senusret II and Senusret III, and against the second pyramid complex Amenemhat III built for himself at Dahshur. Uphill proposed that the Labyrinth was much smaller than the earlier descriptions suggested, consisting of a dock and quay, a valley temple, a causeway, and a mortuary temple with a colonnaded court and a sanctuary containing the royal shrines. He proposed that the causeway was lined on both sides by arrays of three subsidiary pyramids for queens, with walled enclosures containing funerary shrines, passageways and storage chambers, and that these two sets of six structures could correspond to Herodotus's 12 covered courts. He also suggested that Herodotus's reported 1,500 chambers likely included chambers from the mastabas and tombs north-east of the pyramid rather than the mortuary temple complex alone, and that Herodotus may have over-schematised his account.

Two of the popular sources read the site the other way, as confirmation. The Historic Mysteries article says the complex discovered at Hawara matches Herodotus's description both in location and construction, and that it comprises around 3,000 rooms packed with hieroglyphs and murals, just as Herodotus described. The AncientHistoryX write-up, looking back over the geophysical and satellite work that preceded the 2026 dig, describes those prior studies as careful, qualified science that stuck to what the technology could actually detect, which was shallow subsurface features in dry desert soil. That same write-up says the site today looks quiet, showing only the weathered mud-brick core of the pyramid rising from the sand.

File: Labyrinth symbolism and cross-cultural origins

single source

The word 'labyrinth' was not the builders' word. The Historic Mysteries article is careful about this: the builders of the Egyptian complex did not consider it a labyrinth in the modern sense of the word. Herodotus named it as such, after the legendary Greek labyrinth built by Daedalus for King Minos of Crete, where in Greek mythology the minotaur dwelt at the heart of an elaborate maze of tunnels. So the name arrives with Herodotus and carries his cultural frame, not the Egyptians'.

The same article argues that labyrinths were not initially intended to be confusing, merely expansive and complex. It traces the original word to 'labrys', the ceremonial axe used by the Minoan civilization, and says the thing so named was more likely a sprawling palace complex. It backs this with what survives on Crete. Frescoes on the island depict young men acrobatically jumping over charging bulls for sport, and the article says it is not inconceivable to see the origin of the minotaur myth in such daredevil stunts. Minoan palaces were commonly large and had a central open area where events like that could take place, which would put men fighting a giant bull at the heart of the labyrinth. That last step is the article's own suggestion about how the myth may have formed, offered as a possibility rather than as an established fact.

Beyond Crete and Egypt, the article notes that what we call labyrinths have been built throughout history and for many reasons. Malevolent spirits may have been trapped in prehistoric labyrinths, or ritual dances may have taken place in them. Many Roman and Christian labyrinths are found at building entrances, where the design may similarly have been used to ward off evil.

For the cross-cultural picture the article leans on Carl Schuster and Edmund Carpenter, whose 1996 study of signs and symbols, Patterns that Connect: Social Symbolism in Ancient and Tribal Art, ISBN 9780810963269, presents various forms of the labyrinth and suggests various possible intentions behind their construction. One suggestion is that some could form a sacred processional path to the home of a sacred ancestor, and the article compares this to some theories about the Nazca Lines in Peru, adding that the path might also depict the ancestor himself. In some Indian cultures, it says, the labyrinth is regarded as a sacred symbol, a helpful ancestor, and a divinity, which may preserve that original meaning: the ultimate ancestor, evoked by two continuous lines connecting the living to the dead.

Schuster also noticed a second motif running across cultures, the labyrinth as a shelter for a trickster. In India the demon Ravana rules over labyrinths. Among the Sumatran Bataks, the trickster Djonaha dwells in a labyrinth. For Europeans it is often a cage, built to trap whatever monster is hidden at its heart.
