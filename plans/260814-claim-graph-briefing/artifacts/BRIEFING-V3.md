# Why films dont look like films anymore

Everything in this brief comes from the 8 sources on this job. Where something is thin or contested, the sentence says so.

## The short version

Audiences are openly complaining that modern movies look flat, dull, and sludgy despite having the biggest budgets ever, and the sources agree it's not really the CGI or the digital cameras themselves, it's how filmmakers are choosing to use them. Jurassic Park still gets held up as the gold standard because Spielberg used the new technology as little as possible, about 50 fully digital shots total, and hid the rest behind animatronics, rain, and motion blur.

*Reasonably supported; the thin spots are flagged where they occur.*

## Movies with the biggest budgets ever are being called flat, plastic, and cheap-looking

The complaint that starts most of this material is the same one: films now have more money behind them than film has ever had, and yet a lot of them look flat, plastic, and cheap on screen. Some television shows now look more cinematic than what's playing in theaters, a complaint that opens two separate YouTube video essays on the subject, one titled "Why don't movies look like *movies* anymore?" and another titled "Why Movies Don't Look Like Movies Anymore." Why films from the 1980s, 1990s, and early 2000s still look better than what's coming out now is a question one working director of photography wonders aloud in a comment thread of working cinematographers in the Stage 32 Cinematography Lounge. Nobody in this material treats it as a fringe opinion; it is the question each piece sets out to answer.

## CGI and digital cameras are not actually why modern movies look different, according to both major video essays on the topic

The easy answer gets rejected outright by both video essays. One states plainly that digital cameras themselves are not the reason movies look boring. The other calls CGI, visual effects, AI, and filters scapegoats rather than the real cause. Superbad was shot on a Panavision Genesis HD camera with less dynamic range than an iPhone and it still looks good, thanks to choices like a red car in frame and deliberately 1970s-style mahogany-wood, brown-carpeted interiors, an example one essay uses to back this up.

The same argument shows up as a list of counterexamples. Dune, The Substance, Smile, Nosferatu, and The Brutalist are cited by one essayist as films shot in the last few years that look good, with The Brutalist singled out as one of the best-looking films of the decade. On the Stage 32 comment thread, a working filmmaker separately singles out Nosferatu for the same reason, praising director Robert Eggers and cinematographer Jarin Blaschke for creating still frames the audience can sit with and let resonate. The same film comes up independently in two different sources, without either referencing the other. The point both are making is that the tools have not lost the ability to produce a good image; something else is missing.

## Cameras can now capture so much light and shadow that filmmakers show everything, and that is what produces the gray, flat look people are complaining about

The same technical explanation is reached independently by both video essays. One describes it as a "gray sludgy aesthetic" produced by high dynamic range cameras. The other describes filmmakers obsessing over maximizing dynamic range so nothing in the image clips into pure black or pure white, keeping the whole picture inside a narrow exposure band, roughly 10 to 90 percent of the range available.

The argument in both cases is the same: a camera being able to capture everything is not a reason to show everything. Real contrast, like the moment of stepping outside on a bright day and being briefly blinded, is what the human eye actually experiences, and modern digital workflows tend to flatten that experience out rather than preserve it. The same technical description gets reached using different camera specifications and different films as examples, with neither essay appearing to draw on the other.

## Lighting got softer and cameras got closer and shakier, and both changes are described as choices rather than limitations

Softboxes, light domes, china balls, and book lights have replaced the harder, more directional lighting that used to carve shape and texture into a face or a room, a shift one video essay traces in how scenes get lit. Cleaner high-ISO camera sensors let filmmakers skip lighting dim scenes at all, which is why some modern night scenes end up looking closer to night-vision footage than to the deliberately lit blue-tinted night scenes in something like The Lord of the Rings. The same essay makes the point with a blunt comparison: teaching someone to light a green screen takes about 20 minutes, while the industry is said to be losing the patience, or the taste, to light a scene the way David Fincher lit Se7en.

A separate technical argument covers the camera itself. Bigger sensors and wider lenses pull the camera physically closer to actors, breaking the more distant, eye-level framing traditional cinema relied on. Cheap digital recording combined with lightweight gimbals and drones means the camera rarely stops moving, which can make a shot feel closer to a video game than to a composed film frame. A Stage 32 commenter, working independently of that essay, raises the same symptom from a viewer's seat, calling overused shaky cam nauseating.

## When a film is built around thousands of visual effects shots, nobody on set has to commit to a final look, because it might all change later

This idea comes at from several directions. Television shows carrying 2,000 to 3,000 VFX shots make it hard for anyone to commit to a lighting setup, since a room or a window might be altered or removed entirely once the effects team gets to it, producing bland, uncommitted-looking images, as one video essayist points out. A VFX artist writing about The Lost World: Jurassic Park makes a related point about current productions leaning on green screens and virtual production volumes, arguing it can leave actors feeling like they are not actually standing in an environment.

The Stage 32 comment thread adds a production detail: colorists and post-production houses can sometimes undo a carefully shot image after the fact, which is why some cinematographers now insist on sitting in on their own color-grading sessions. This shift, from committing to an image on set to fixing it later in post-production, is a major reason modern films feel fake even when they're shot on real locations, according to a separate article on the philosophy of Jurassic World Rebirth.

## Some working cinematographers argue this is really about financiers and algorithms, not artistic laziness

This argument appears in one place, the Stage 32 comment thread, but it directly challenges every craft-based explanation offered elsewhere. One commenter argues that the discussion of the video everyone is reacting to leaves out the business side entirely, and that today's visual style is shaped more by financiers, the people controlling the budget, and by algorithms than by the creative decisions of directors and cinematographers. Another commenter, Maurice Vaughan, agrees and adds a specific comparison: indie films often look as good as older studio films precisely because indie cinematographers have more creative freedom than people working inside the studio system.

The implication is a direct counter to the idea that filmmakers simply stopped trying. If a cinematographer working outside the studio system, with the same lenses and the same cameras, can still produce a strong image, the missing ingredient may not be skill or effort at all. It may be who is allowed to make the decision.

## Jurassic Park kept its digital dinosaurs to about 50 shots and hid nearly all of them in rain, dusk, or motion blur, and the material argues modern films do the opposite

The most repeated fact across this material is that Steven Spielberg originally planned to shoot Jurassic Park using only practical effects, stop-motion raptors and a full-size animatronic Tyrannosaurus rex, and only added computer-generated dinosaurs after Industrial Light & Magic showed him test footage. The finished film landed at roughly 50 shots of fully digital creatures, including what is described as the first full-body digital dinosaur shot ever committed to film, the Brachiosaurus. A VFX artist writing separately about the sequel, The Lost World, notes that late-1990s CGI simply was not advanced enough to lean on heavily, which is why even a visceral moment like the T-Rex's tongue was done practically rather than digitally, and why the team balanced practical work with sparing digital shots.

A film blog looking back at the original film explains what that scarcity bought the production: because there were so few fully digital shots, the effects team could lavish attention on each individual frame, and they hid the weaker ones in low light. The Tyrannosaurus chasing the jeep happens in rain. The Tyrannosaurus fighting the raptors happens inside a dim park building. The brachiosaurs are shown at sunset. The one major effects sequence shot in full daylight, the gallimimus stampede, hides its seams with speed and motion blur instead of darkness.

The same VFX artist argues that modern productions do close to the opposite. Movies that lean on CGI for every shot place a heavy burden on artists working under tight deadlines, and spreading artists across thousands of shots tends to lower overall quality, because the kind of meticulous, shot-by-shot planning the original Jurassic Park crew did gets abandoned. The film blog adds that modern blockbusters using CGI in nearly every frame do not get the low-light or motion-blur cover Jurassic Park used, so their effects have to hold up in plain view, with nowhere left to hide.

## If audiences no longer punish bad effects, that removes one of the pressures that used to force careful work, which lines up with the claim that financiers now drive the visual style

One film blog argues that audience tolerance for CGI has changed the incentives. By the time of writing, audiences had grown so used to computer-generated imagery that even weak effects work, in something like The Avengers or The Hobbit, does not cost those films at the box office; the flaws just fade into the background. Back in 1993 there was no such acceptance, so the Jurassic Park team had to spend maximum time, money, and attention getting the dinosaurs right, because a bad effect then would have been a real problem. This claim comes from a single blog post with no box office data or audience survey behind it; it is an assertion about audience behavior, not a measurement of it.

Separately, a Stage 32 comment thread argues that today's visual style is driven more by financiers and algorithms than by the creative choices of directors and cinematographers, and that indie films often look better than studio films specifically because indie cinematographers have more freedom from that system. No source puts these two claims side by side, but they fit together in an obvious way: if audiences no longer punish a flat or sloppy image, the people controlling a film's budget have less market pressure to insist on the kind of obsessive, shot-by-shot craft the Jurassic Park team applied. That connection isn't made explicitly by either source; it remains an inference rather than something either piece argues directly.

## Two different explanations for why modern footage feels less real are never weighed against each other in this material

One explanation is technical and about exposure: modern cameras can capture such a wide range of light and shadow that filmmakers choose to show all of it, keeping the image inside a narrow, evenly-lit exposure band instead of letting parts of the frame go properly dark or properly bright, which produces the flat, gray look several sources describe. The argument is that a camera being capable of capturing everything is not a reason to display everything, since real human vision includes genuine contrast, like being briefly blinded on stepping outside into sunlight.

A separate, more philosophical explanation, covered in an article about one video essayist's argument, says the realness gap has less to do with exposure and more to do with deep focus and the physical difference between film and a digital sensor: older films kept backgrounds sharp so the eye could scan a frame the way it scans a real room, and celluloid is treated as more authentic because light physically struck the film stock, while a digital sensor only converts light into data. That argument is made concrete by comparing the sweaty, straining actors of Sorcerer, a 1977 film starring Roy Scheider, to a similarly humid scene in Jurassic World Rebirth, where flat lighting and clean makeup leave the actors looking comfortable rather than authentic.

Modern footage feeling less real is the complaint both explanations are trying to account for, though neither source engages with the other's argument. It is possible both mechanisms are operating on the same footage at once, one about exposure and one about focus and material, but nothing in this material tries to reconcile them or say which one is doing more of the work.

## Things that stood out

- A single film blog, "Four Reasons Why Jurassic Park's Visuals Still Amaze," supplies some of the sharpest details in the whole set, the rain-and-dusk hiding technique, the gallimimus motion-blur trick, and the theory about audience tolerance for bad CGI, and none of these appear in any other source, though the underlying events are independently checkable.
- The claim that modern cinematography can still look good rests heavily on one film, Nosferatu, which two different sources praise independently, but the broader list of currently good-looking films, Dune, The Substance, Smile, The Brutalist, comes from only one of those two sources.
- The argument that financiers and algorithms, not artists, are driving the flat modern look never names an actual financier, studio, or algorithm; it describes an unnamed mechanism rather than a documented one.
- The scarcity of Jurassic Park's digital effects is measured in a specific number, about 50 shots, while the modern comparison point is a television show's 2,000 to 3,000 VFX shots; the two numbers come from different kinds of productions made decades apart and are never lined up against a modern feature film's own shot count.

## How this topic is usually covered, and what is not

The same shape shows up across every piece in this material: it opens with a viewer or working cinematographer noticing that modern films look flat or plastic despite bigger budgets, rules out CGI and digital cameras as the simple explanation, then offers a technical or workflow-based reason, dynamic range, lighting choices, lens and camera movement, or fixing things in post, before turning to Jurassic Park as the example of how things used to be done, repeating the roughly 50-digital-shots figure and the practical-effects-first story almost word for word across separate outlets. Business and audience-behavior explanations appear only at the margins, in a comment thread and a single blog post, rather than as a throughline any piece builds around.

No source in this material includes a current VFX artist or supervisor explaining what a typical shot count or deadline actually looks like on a modern blockbuster, so the claim that artists are spread too thin is asserted from outside rather than confirmed by anyone doing the work. No working director of photography defending a high-dynamic-range or green-screen-heavy approach appears anywhere, so every technical explanation here is critical rather than answered. Nobody with a studio or financing background explains how a modern tentpole's visual style actually gets approved, even though one source blames financiers directly. There is no side-by-side shot count or budget comparison between Jurassic Park and a modern film of similar scale, so the contrast rests on one specific number against a vague one. How audiences now watch films, on phones, laptops, or home HDR televisions, and whether grading for many screen types at once might itself be shaping the flatter look everyone describes, is a question no source raises.

## What is solid and what is thin

**Solid, safe to state plainly:**
- Everybody's noticing the same thing: movies with the biggest budgets ever look flat, plastic, and cheap.
- The two main video essays agree: CGI, digital cameras, and AI are not actually why movies look different now.
- Modern cameras can capture so much light and shadow now that filmmakers show everything, and that's what makes the image look flat and gray.
- Once a movie is built around thousands of VFX shots and green screens, nobody on set commits to a final look anymore, because it might all get changed later.
- Jurassic Park is the film everyone points to as the gold standard, and the reason given again and again is that Spielberg barely used CGI at all.

**Thin, attribute these if you use them:**
- Some cinematographers think this whole thing is really about money and algorithms, not artistic choices at all. (one source only)
- Because there were so few CGI shots, the crew could obsess over each one, and they hid the weak ones in rain, dusk, and motion blur. (one source only)
- In 1993 audiences would not have forgiven bad dinosaurs, but today's audiences are so used to CGI that mediocre effects barely hurt a movie. (one source only)
- The 'realness' gap has less to do with how much CGI a film uses and more to do with deep focus, film grain, and literal light hitting film versus a sensor converting it, one video essay argues. (one source only)

**Open questions nobody in the sources answers:**
- nobody actually asked a VFX artist working on a current blockbuster why crews are stretched thin or why studios greenlight thousand-shot movies the whole 'artists are spread too thin' claim is asserted by outside writers, not by anyone who's actually sat in that chair, so a sharp viewer will ask how anyone would know
- no cinematographer or director working today defending the soft, flat modern look with their own reasoning right now every voice in this video is a critic, so it plays as one-sided even if the criticism is fair, and a real pushback would actually make the argument stronger
- nobody from the studio or financing side is in here explaining the business logic behind these choices claim 8 blames financiers and algorithms but only ever hears from cinematographers complaining about them, so it's a one-sided account of a fight we only see one side of
- no actual shot-count or budget comparison between Jurassic Park and a modern blockbuster of similar scale the whole 'they used fewer shots and had more time' argument would hit so much harder with real numbers side by side instead of 'about 50 shots' from one movie and vague 'thousands' from another

**Where those answers would likely be found:**
- Find a VFX artist or supervisor, maybe through r/vfx or a trade outlet like Cartoon Brew, and just ask them what a typical shot count and deadline looks like on a modern tentpole.
- Look for interviews with a current blockbuster's DP about their color grading choices or camera pick, ideally someone explaining an HDR-first workflow.
- Chase a producer or studio executive interview, or a trade piece in Variety or The Hollywood Reporter about how a modern tentpole's visual style gets approved.
- Look for a making-of book or VFX trade retrospective, like Cinefex archives, that lists actual shot counts and schedules for Jurassic Park and a comparable modern film.
