# Why films don't look like films anymore

**Read this like I'm telling you what I found, because that's what it is.** Everything here comes from the 8 sources on this job: two video essays, five articles, and a forum thread. Where something's shaky, I say so in the sentence. No homework required to trust it.

---

## The whole thing in one breath

People keep saying modern movies look flat and plastic, and they're not imagining it. The sources trace it to a pile of choices that all push the same direction: cameras built to capture every shade of light, wide lenses chasing blurry backgrounds, soft lighting everywhere, cameras that never stop moving, and a habit of fixing everything in post instead of committing on set. Underneath all of it, money and brutal VFX schedules. And the reason everyone keeps bringing up Jurassic Park is that it's the perfect counter-example: it barely used its groundbreaking CGI, hid what it did use in the dark and the rain, and let animatronics and story carry the weight. That's the whole territory. Everything below is the detail.

---

## The threads

There are really five stories tangled together in this pile, not one.

**1. The complaint itself.** Two separate video essayists sat down, studied the same problem, and landed on almost the same words: flat, plastic, sludgy. That's the strongest thing in the whole pile, because they got there without each other. But it's a *feeling* they're describing, not a measurement — nobody in these sources has actually measured anything. Worth knowing: one essay admits the whole thing might be generational, that younger viewers might look at the old high-contrast style and see something glitchy and dated. That's not a footnote, that's a real crack in the premise, and pretending it isn't there would be dishonest.

**2. The camera did it.** This is the most solid thread. Both essays name the same mechanism: modern cameras chase maximum dynamic range, and grabbing every shade of light flattens the punch out of the image. It's the most concrete, checkable claim here — an actual engineering choice you can point at. Around it hang a few one-source cousins: soft lighting that no longer carves shape into anything, wide lenses and bokeh changing how close and flat shots feel, and a nice theory that old deep-focus shots feel "real" because they match how your eyes actually work. Those cousins are each one person's argument. Good material, but you'd be carrying them alone.

**3. Nobody commits anymore.** Digital freed filmmakers from physical limits — the camera never has to stop moving, and every decision can be deferred to post. One source argues that freedom quietly killed the discipline that made old shots feel deliberate. The wildest version of this thread, and it's one source only: even the *old classics* are getting flattened, because remasters run them through the same modern pipeline. If that holds up, it's the scariest sentence in the pile — it means it's not a style choice, it's a skill disappearing.

**4. It's the money.** Tighter budgets, crunched VFX schedules, cinematographers squeezed out of the post-production room where the look now actually gets decided. A forum thread and an article both land here. Notice something though: this thread *explains* threads 2 and 3. If schedules and budgets drive the choices, then the camera stuff isn't laziness or bad taste, it's downstream of economics. That connection is the closest thing this pile has to an actual thesis.

**5. The Jurassic Park counter-example.** Everything above is the disease; this is the movie everyone holds up as the cure. It still looks real because it used its own revolutionary CGI *sparingly* — animatronics on set, computers only when nothing physical could do the job. And here's the detail that made me stop: they actively hid the CGI they did use. The big dinosaur scenes happen at night, in rain, behind motion blur — that's not mood, that's the effects team covering their tracks. There's a fun little dispute here too: one writer says the effects had to be perfect because 1993 audiences had never seen anything like it, and a reader shot back with a much simpler explanation. Small, but it's a live argument you could stage. The legacy claim is rock solid across every source: the film changed how the industry used CGI *and* kicked off a genuine boom in dinosaur science — even though its own dinosaur facts were wrong. Everyone loves that irony, including me.

## Where the threads touch

This is the part worth staring at.

- **Money explains machinery.** Thread 4 sits underneath threads 2 and 3. Stitch them and the story stops being "filmmakers got lazy" and becomes "the economics changed and the image followed." Nobody in the sources says this outright in one place — it's sitting there between two sources, unassembled.
- **The remaster claim connects everything.** If modern pipelines flatten even old films (thread 3), then the problem isn't taste, cameras, or laziness — it's the pipeline itself. One source, huge implication, nobody else touches it. That's either the weakest claim in the pile or the most interesting one, and honestly it's both.
- **The generational pushback quietly undercuts the whole premise.** If "flat" is just what new looks like to old eyes, threads 2 through 4 are explaining a problem that doesn't exist. None of the sources take this seriously enough to answer it. A video that *did* take it seriously for two minutes would instantly feel more honest than every source in this pile.
- **Jurassic Park is doing double duty.** It's both the "practical effects age better" example everyone uses *and* a restraint story almost nobody tells: the most famous CGI movie ever made is famous partly for how little CGI it has, and for how deliberately it hid the rest. The first version is worn out. The second one isn't.

## The stuff that made me stop

Little things that could be something, or nothing.

- The dinosaur scenes happening in rain and darkness *on purpose*. Once you see it you can't unsee it, and it reframes "movie magic" as sleight of hand.
- The claim that remasters are flattening old classics. One source. If it's checkable, it changes the whole argument.
- "More powerful tools bought less creativity" — one essayist's argument, but it rhymes with every thread in the pile.
- The reader who punctured the "1993 audiences demanded perfection" theory with something simpler. Small, human, and exactly the kind of pushback that makes a video feel honest.

## If you end up talking about this out loud

The safe ground: two essays independently landing on "flat and plastic," the dynamic-range mechanism, everything about how Jurassic Park was actually made, and the science-boom legacy. Say those with your chest.

The careful ground: everything that traces to one source — the soft lighting, the bokeh argument, the remaster claim, the "digital overshot film" theory. Use them, they're good — just say "one essay argues" instead of asserting them, because if someone pushes back, that's all you've got.

The honest-on-camera moments, if you want them: nobody in these sources ever asked a working VFX artist about the crunch everyone blames, and no cinematographer gets to defend the look they're being accused of choosing. The whole prosecution rests, and the defense never takes the stand. Said out loud, that's not a weakness in your video — that's the most trustworthy sentence in it.

The one thing genuinely missing if you chase this further: numbers. The Jurassic Park vs. modern blockbuster comparison runs entirely on vibes in every source here. Shot counts, budgets, schedules — a making-of book or a VFX trade retrospective would turn the best argument in this pile from a feeling into a fact.
