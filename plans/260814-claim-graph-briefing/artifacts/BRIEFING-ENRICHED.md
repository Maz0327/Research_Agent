# Why films dont look like films anymore and use jurassic park as an example

Everything in this brief comes from the 8 sources on this job. Where something is thin or contested, the sentence says so.

## The short version

Audiences are openly complaining that modern movies look flat, dull, and sludgy despite having the biggest budgets ever, and the sources agree it's not really the CGI or the digital cameras themselves, it's how filmmakers are choosing to use them. Jurassic Park still gets held up as the gold standard because Spielberg used the new technology as little as possible, about 50 fully digital shots total, and hid the rest behind animatronics, rain, and motion blur.

*Reasonably supported; the thin spots are flagged where they occur.*

## How movies look right now is drawing the same complaint from audiences, video essayists, and working cinematographers, all independently

Movies today have bigger budgets than film has ever had, yet a lot of people say the pictures look flat, plastic, and cheap. Some even say certain television shows look more cinematic than what's currently playing in theaters. Why do movies from the 1980s, 1990s, and early 2000s look better than what's coming out now? That question opens two separate YouTube video essays, and a comment thread among working cinematographers on the industry site Stage 32 raises it independently too. None of these three sources appear to be quoting or citing each other. They arrive at the same starting point separately, which is worth noting, since it means this isn't one person's pet peeve.

## The people making these arguments go out of their way to say CGI, digital cameras, and AI are not actually the cause

The easy answer gets rejected in both video essays before they make their real argument. One says plainly that digital cameras themselves are not why movies look boring. The other calls CGI, visual effects, AI, and filters scapegoats rather than the real cause, and backs it with an example: Superbad was shot on the Panavision Genesis, a digital camera with less dynamic range than an iPhone, but it still looks good because of deliberate choices, a red car, and 1970s-style interiors with mahogany wood paneling and brown carpet. The camera was unremarkable. The choices made with it were not.

The same point gets made a second way, by pointing at films that look good right now. Dune, The Substance, Smile, Nosferatu, and The Brutalist prove that modern cinematography can absolutely look good, according to one essayist, who calls The Brutalist one of the best-looking films of the decade. Separately, on the Stage 32 thread, a working filmmaker singles out Nosferatu for the same reason, praising director Robert Eggers and cinematographer Jarin Blaschke for creating still frames worth sitting with. The same film turns up as the counterexample in two unconnected sources. If the tools themselves were the problem, films made with the same tools wouldn't look this different from each other. The skill hasn't disappeared, it just isn't being applied everywhere.

## Modern cameras can capture so much light and shadow now that filmmakers are showing everything, and that is what makes the image look flat and gray

This same technical point turns up independently in two essays, just phrased slightly differently. One describes a 'gray sludgy aesthetic' created by high-dynamic-range cameras. The other describes filmmakers obsessing over maximizing dynamic range so nothing clips, keeping the whole image inside a narrow 10 to 90 percent exposure range instead of letting parts of the frame go properly dark or bright.

The underlying argument in both cases is the same: just because a camera can capture everything doesn't mean a film should show everything. Real contrast, the kind you experience stepping outside into bright sun after being indoors, is part of how the human eye actually sees. Flattening that range so nothing is lost in post-production is also flattening the image's sense of depth and drama.

## Lighting got softer and camera work got looser, trading deliberate choices for faster defaults

Lighting has shifted from harsh, hard sources that carved out shape and texture to softboxes, domes, china balls, and book lights, a softer default, one essay describes. It also points out that cleaner high-ISO sensors now let filmmakers skip lighting dim scenes altogether, which is why some modern night scenes look like night-vision footage rather than the deliberately lit blue-tinted night scenes in something like Lord of the Rings. The industry is losing the taste to light a scene the way David Fincher lit Se7en, a second essay argues from a different angle, noting that teaching someone to light a green screen takes about twenty minutes, a rough measure of how much less deliberate effort now goes into lighting a shot.

Camera movement and lens choice tell a similar story. Bigger sensors and wider lenses pull the camera physically closer to actors, breaking the more distant, eye-level framing traditional cinema used. Cheap digital recording combined with lightweight gimbals and drones means cameras rarely stop moving, which can make a shot feel more like a video game than cinema. This detailed technical account comes from one essay, but it lines up with a blunter, independent complaint on the Stage 32 thread, where a commenter separately calls out overused shaky camera work as nauseating.

## Once a movie leans on thousands of visual-effects shots, nobody on set has to commit to a final look, because it might all change later

This same mechanism shows up from four separate directions that don't reference each other. Television shows built around 2,000 to 3,000 visual-effects shots make it hard to commit to lighting on set, one video essayist points out, because rooms and windows might be altered or removed entirely in post-production, leading to bland, uncommitted images. A visual-effects artist writing about The Lost World: Jurassic Park makes almost the same point about modern movies leaning on green screens and virtual-production volumes, saying it can leave actors feeling like they aren't actually standing in an environment.

The Stage 32 comment thread adds a related piece: colorists and post-production houses can sometimes wreck a good image after the fact, which is why some working cinematographers now insist on sitting in on their own color-grading sessions rather than leaving it to be finished without them. And a more philosophy-flavored piece about Jurassic World Rebirth argues that this general shift, from committing to an image on set to fixing it later, is a big part of why modern films feel fake even when they're shot on real locations rather than in front of a green screen.

A documentary-style video essayist, a visual-effects artist, a thread of working cinematographers, and a philosophy-focused essay all describe the same underlying problem from their own vantage point, without citing each other. None of them puts it together as one mechanism explicitly; that's an assembly made across the four accounts, not something any single one of them states.

## Jurassic Park is held up as the gold standard, and the reason given again and again is that Spielberg used digital effects as little as possible

This point is the most repeated one across the material. A visual-effects artist writing about the sequel, The Lost World, explains that late-1990s computer graphics simply weren't advanced enough to use heavily, so Spielberg balanced practical effects with sparing digital work rather than leaning on CGI by default. Two separate history-focused articles describe the same production choice in more detail: Spielberg originally planned to use only practical effects, stop-motion raptors and an animatronic Tyrannosaurus rex, and only added digital dinosaurs after Industrial Light and Magic showed him test footage that changed his mind. The finished film ended up with about fifty shots of fully digital creatures, including what's described as the first full-body digital dinosaur ever put on screen, the Brachiosaurus. A film blog looking back twenty years later agrees, saying the filmmakers relied primarily on animatronics and used CGI only for creatures that couldn't be built practically. The same story and the same number turn up across four sources made for very different audiences: a visual-effects piece, two history overviews, and a film blog.

Because there were so few digital shots, the crew could give unusual attention to each one, and the film blog lays out how the effects team hid the more difficult ones in low light: the Tyrannosaurus chasing the jeep in a rainstorm, the Tyrannosaurus fighting the raptors in the dim park headquarters building, the brachiosaurs shown at sunset. The one major effects sequence shot in full daylight, the gallimimus stampede, hides its seams with speed and motion blur rather than darkness. This detail comes from a single film blog, though a reader comment on that same post adds a supporting comparison, noting that Disney's Dinosaur ride hides its animatronics in the dark for the same reason.

## Modern movies reverse that ratio: computer-generated effects appear everywhere, often in full daylight, spread across artists working under tight deadlines

A visual-effects artist argues that movies which lean on CGI for nearly everything place a heavy burden on artists working under tight schedules, spreading them thin across thousands of shots, and that this tends to lower overall quality, because the kind of meticulous, shot-by-shot planning the original Jurassic Park team did gets abandoned when there's simply too much to plan for. A film blog adds a related point: modern blockbusters with computer-generated imagery in nearly every frame don't get the low-light or motion-blur cover Jurassic Park used, so their effects have to hold up in plain view, with nowhere to hide.

The same film blog offers a further explanation for why this doesn't seem to cost studios much: it argues that by 2013, audiences had grown so used to CGI that even weak effects work fades into the background, so mediocre effects in something like The Avengers or The Hobbit don't cost those films at the box office. Back in 1993, it argues, no such tolerance existed, so the Jurassic Park team had to spend maximum time, energy, and money getting the dinosaurs exactly right, because a bad effect then would have been a real problem for the film. This whole argument about audience tolerance rests on one blog post's assertion, without any box-office figures or survey data cited to support it.

## Jurassic Park's habit of hiding its digital shots in rain and shadow is a concrete example of the exact discipline the modern-film critiques say has disappeared

The same underlying craft shows up in two groups of sources, even though they never discuss the same film. On one side, the Jurassic Park retrospective material describes a specific tactic: because there were only about fifty fully digital shots in the film, the crew could hide the harder ones in low light and motion, the Tyrannosaurus chasing the jeep in rain, the Tyrannosaurus and raptors fighting in a dim building, the brachiosaurs at sunset, with only the daylight gallimimus stampede covered by speed and blur instead of darkness.

On the other side, the essays diagnosing why current movies look flat describe a general loss of exactly that kind of restraint. One explains that modern cameras capture so much dynamic range that filmmakers show everything evenly instead of using real contrast the way the eye actually experiences light. Another describes lighting shifting away from hard, deliberate sources that carve out shape and texture, toward flatter, softer defaults that require less planning.

Put together, Jurassic Park's rain, dusk, and motion blur were a specific application of exactly the kind of deliberate, shape-carving, uneven lighting and exposure that the modern-craft essays say has been replaced by flatter, more even images. Neither side makes this connection itself: the Jurassic Park retrospective pieces never mention the modern dynamic-range or lighting arguments, and the modern-film essays never bring up Jurassic Park's specific hiding techniques.

## Two other explanations sit outside the main craft argument and don't fully reconcile with it

One is about money rather than skill. On the Stage 32 comment thread, one commenter argues that the video everyone is discussing doesn't account enough for the business side, and that today's visual style is driven more by financiers and algorithms than by creative people making choices. A second commenter agrees and adds that indie films often look as good as older movies specifically because indie cinematographers have more creative freedom than people working inside the studio system. This directly complicates the idea that filmmakers simply got lazy or lost their taste: if indie films made with the same tools still look good, the problem may be less about craft and more about who is allowed to make the decisions. This argument comes only from anonymous comments on one thread, not from either of the video essays that frame the wider question.

The second is more philosophical. The gap in 'realness' isn't really about how much CGI a film uses at all, according to a single article summarizing one YouTube essayist. It claims older films used more deep focus, keeping backgrounds sharp so the eye could scan the frame the way it scans a real room, while modern shallow-focus shots don't work that way. It also argues that celluloid feels authentic because light physically struck the film, while a digital sensor just converts light into data. To make the point concrete, it compares the sweaty, struggling actors in Sorcerer, the 1977 film starring Roy Scheider, to a similarly humid, tropical scene in Jurassic World Rebirth, where flat lighting and clean makeup make the actors look comfortable rather than authentic. This entire argument comes from one article about one video essay, and nothing else in the material engages with these specific ideas about deep focus or film grain; it's possible both this argument and the dynamic-range and lighting arguments described elsewhere are true at once, but no source tries to connect them.

## Things that stood out

- The claim about Jurassic Park hiding its CGI shots in rain and dusk, and the separate claim that audiences by 2013 had grown too tolerant of bad effects to punish The Avengers or The Hobbit, both come from the same single film blog. They read as two independent pieces of evidence but are really one writer's argument told in two parts.
- Nosferatu is named as a modern film that still looks like a film by two completely unconnected sources, a video essayist and a filmmaker commenting on an industry thread, without either referencing the other.
- The pushback that this might be about financiers and algorithms rather than creative laziness comes only from anonymous comments on one article; neither of the two video essays that set up the whole 'why don't movies look like movies' question engages with that possibility.
- Jurassic Park's digital effects get a specific figure everywhere, about fifty shots, but modern effects-heavy movies never get an equivalent number for a current blockbuster, so the comparison ends up as a precise figure set against a vague one.

## How this topic is usually covered, and what is not

The sources split into two camps that don't talk to each other. Video essays and cinematographer comment threads diagnose what changed in cinematography generally, dynamic range, lighting, lens choice, reliance on post-production, using modern films as symptom examples. Separately, Jurassic Park retrospective articles and a visual-effects piece on its sequel celebrate that film's practical-effects approach on its own terms, as a story about one production rather than as evidence in the wider argument about why films look different now.

No source explicitly connects Jurassic Park's specific hiding techniques, rain, dusk, motion blur, to the modern-craft complaints about flat dynamic range and soft lighting, even though they describe the same kind of restraint. No source offers actual production data, shot counts, budgets, or schedules, comparing Jurassic Park to a modern blockbuster of similar scale; the fifty-shot figure for Jurassic Park sits next to only vague references to modern films' 'thousands' of shots. And nobody with actual decision-making power on the business or technical side, a studio executive, a financier, or a colorist grading for multiple screen formats, appears anywhere in the material; every voice is a critic, an essayist, or a cinematographer complaining about choices made by people who aren't in the room.

## What is solid and what is thin

**Solid, safe to state plainly:**
- Everybody's noticing the same thing: movies with the biggest budgets ever look flat, plastic, and cheap.
- The two main video essays agree: CGI, digital cameras, and AI are not actually why movies look different now.
- Modern cameras can capture so much light and shadow now that filmmakers show everything, and that's what makes the image look flat and gray.
- Once a movie is built around thousands of VFX shots and green screens, nobody on set commits to a final look anymore, because it might all get changed later.
- Jurassic Park is the film everyone points to as the gold standard, and the reason given again and again is that Spielberg barely used CGI at all.

**Thin, attribute these if you use them:**
- Some cinematographers think this whole thing is really about money and algorithms, not artistic choices at all. (one source only)
- Because there were so few CGI shots, the crew could obsess over each one, and they hid the weak ones in rain, dusk, and motion blur. (one source only)
- In 1993 audiences would not have forgiven bad dinosaurs, but today's audiences are so used to CGI that mediocre effects barely hurt a movie. (one source only)
- The 'realness' gap has less to do with how much CGI a film uses and more to do with deep focus, film grain, and literal light hitting film versus a sensor converting it, one video essay argues. (one source only)

**Open questions nobody in the sources answers:**
- nobody actually asked a VFX artist working on a current blockbuster why crews are stretched thin or why studios greenlight thousand-shot movies the whole 'artists are spread too thin' claim is asserted by outside writers, not by anyone who's actually sat in that chair, so a sharp viewer will ask how anyone would know
- no cinematographer or director working today defending the soft, flat modern look with their own reasoning right now every voice in this video is a critic, so it plays as one-sided even if the criticism is fair, and a real pushback would actually make the argument stronger
- nobody from the studio or financing side is in here explaining the business logic behind these choices claim 8 blames financiers and algorithms but only ever hears from cinematographers complaining about them, so it's a one-sided account of a fight we only see one side of
- no actual shot-count or budget comparison between Jurassic Park and a modern blockbuster of similar scale the whole 'they used fewer shots and had more time' argument would hit so much harder with real numbers side by side instead of 'about 50 shots' from one movie and vague 'thousands' from another

**Where those answers would likely be found:**
- Find a VFX artist or supervisor, maybe through r/vfx or a trade outlet like Cartoon Brew, and just ask them what a typical shot count and deadline looks like on a modern tentpole.
- Look for interviews with a current blockbuster's DP about their color grading choices or camera pick, ideally someone explaining an HDR-first workflow.
- Chase a producer or studio executive interview, or a trade piece in Variety or The Hollywood Reporter about how a modern tentpole's visual style gets approved.
- Look for a making-of book or VFX trade retrospective, like Cinefex archives, that lists actual shot counts and schedules for Jurassic Park and a comparable modern film.
