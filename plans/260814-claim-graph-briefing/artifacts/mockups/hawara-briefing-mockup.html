What you've got: five videos — a Joe Rogan clip with a guest laying out the whole scan saga, a Sphinx conspiracy video, a Why Files episode, and two videos from the same creator (Johanna), one of which is an interview with Trevor Grassi of the Archaeological Rescue Foundation — plus Grassi's own foundation mega-page, the believers' substack, two Wikipedia articles, Herodotus (fetch failed), a serious archaeology magazine feature, a mystery site, two Khafre-Project debunks, and coverage of the 2026 dig. On paper that's sixteen sources. It isn't. The entire affirmative case flows through one small crew — De Cordier, Grassi, Brown, Akers, Boulter — who fund each other's scans, publish on each other's platforms, and appear in each other's videos. Their own flagship document says it proudly: "at least one member of our current extended team was present at every one" of the four scanning missions. That's the most important thing in the pile — what looks like four independent confirmations is one network scanning the same spot four times.

The story, assembled: the ancient part is real and unusually strong. Six classical authors describe a colossal building at Hawara; Herodotus and Strabo actually walked it, and Herodotus counted 3,000 chambers — half above ground, half below, the lower half off-limits. Even the most skeptical source in the pile treats the building as historical fact. In 1888 Flinders Petrie found a giant artificial stone bed there — a thousand feet long — and concluded he was standing on the foundation of a labyrinth quarried to nothing. That's been the textbook position ever since. The believers' whole case is one elegant inversion: what if Petrie found the roof? Three ancient authors do say the roof was stone slabs "like the walls." The 2008 Mataha expedition — De Cordier's money, Egypt's own NRIAG doing the geophysics, Ghent University attached — scanned below the slab and reported a grid of granite-like walls at 8–12 meters. Then the results went quiet: De Cordier says Hawass's council banned all communication citing national security, threatened the team, and blacklisted him when he self-published in 2010. After that the claims escalate with each retelling: Boulter's satellite work adds a flooded level and a dry one; Akers's 2015 Merlin Burrows scan adds four levels, a dome the size of Hagia Sophia, two intact wooden boats, and a 40-meter freestanding metal object at the dead center. Note the pattern: the two people who saw the wildest data, Akers and Boulter, are both dead, and Akers's scan sat under a ten-year NDA until 2025 — every extraordinary claim routes through witnesses who can no longer be questioned.

Three real fights. Still there vs. long gone: the scan network vs. Petrie and the mainstream — and the telling detail is that the best scholarly piece in the pile never mentions the 2008 scans at all. It's busy with a more boring problem: the ancient accounts contradict each other, only two authors actually visited, and the Egyptologist Eric Uphill's reconstruction dissolves the "one giant building" into an ordinary pyramid complex that merely felt like a maze. One mid-tier source does bridge the lanes — dishonestly: it claims the 2008 radar found rooms "packed with magnificent hieroglyphs and murals." Radar cannot see murals. Suppression vs. disclosure politics: the cover-up story has a hole its own tellers keep stepping in — Egypt granted the permits, Egypt's own institute ran and published the scans, and in May 2026 Egypt launched an official dig at Hawara, the thing a real cover-up would never allow. The Rogan guest supplies the better theory himself: "I kind of don't really blame him… it was a political decision" — Egypt controls who announces. The fight isn't truth vs. lies; it's who owns the reveal. The Khafre problem: Snopes and Egyptian Streets dismantle the 2025 "city under Giza" claims — no peer review, radar physically can't reach those depths, the viral image AI-generated — and the Hawara crew welded themselves to that exact team: Biondi of the Khafre Project is announced as Hawara's next scanner. The debunkers never mention Hawara, but their ammunition transfers directly, and the believers loaded it for them.

What's honestly missing: the actual scan data — the NRIAG papers and Mataha whitepaper are linked inside these sources but never quoted, so the entire pile is descriptions of evidence, not evidence; Hawass's side of the Mataha story in any form; a single independent expert speaking about Hawara's scans specifically; and anything on the 2026 dig from anyone outside the believer network.

  • 1st c. BC–43 ADDiodorus, Strabo, Pliny, Mela describe it; details conflict (courts, storeys, builder). Strabo visits; calls the roof single stones. SRC_11 · 16
    More

    The conflicts matter later: Diodorus calls it the tomb of a king "Mendes" and says the Cretan labyrinth was a copy; Strabo counts courts by nomes ("fewer than 30") and insists the building is one storey; Pliny reports a "temple of the Sun" theory, doors that thunder when opened, and — crucially — that locals had been quarrying it "with extraordinary hatred" for centuries; Mela says 12 palaces and 1,000 houses. Same building, four different buildings' worth of description — the scholarly case against a reliable reconstruction starts here.

Point of the entry: three centuries ago something was still visible above ground that is entirely gone today.

He read bed-plus-chips as floor-plus-demolition-debris, reconstructed the building on paper from the ancient texts, and closed the case: quarried away. File: The 2008 Scans

documented single network on interpretation

Two overlapping missions, often conflated. Brown's Wroclaw/Cairo project (2007–9) drilled boreholes for groundwater, GPR-scanned the pyramid's flanks, found the structure's true corner deep under the sand, and located what the network calls the entrance shaft descending below the pyramid. De Cordier's Mataha expedition (Feb–Mar 2008) had Dr. Abbas of NRIAG run GPR, VLF-EM, resistivity and seismic tomography over Petrie's stone bed. Reported result: below the slab, at 8–12 m, a grid structure "of gigantic size" in very high-resistivity material "like granite," walls meters thick, on both sides of the canal, out of angle with the pyramid by 20–25° — which Johanna's video (SRC_4) takes as a hint the labyrinth predates the pyramid. Findings appeared in NRIAG's fall 2008 journal and a Ghent lecture. The corpus never reproduces a single scan image or data plot; the whitepaper and two Abbas papers exist as links only. Independent radar experts have not addressed these specific results anywhere in the pile.

File: The Water

established

The least contested fact and the engine of the 2026 story. A canal cut across the site around 1820 (Bahr Wahbi offshoot); the pyramid entrance now sits under ~6 m of water; Petrie's 19th-century access is impossible. Groundwater is saline and rising — a 2007 Geoarchaeology paper studied it formally, and Abbas published an updated water-level report in 2024. Scans are read as showing upper sub-levels flooded, deeper ones possibly dry. The rescue plan: patch the canal with cement and root-buffer planting short-term, reroute it entirely long-term, then drain, excavate, and photogrammetry-map everything before touching an artifact. Cost "well into the millions," years of work. Both believers and the official dig agree on the water; it's the one place the whole corpus points the same way.

Disputed & Uncertain

Every genuine disagreement, staged with its holders — never flattened into consensus, never resolved unless the sources resolve it. The chip is the claim's actual status in the corpus, not a vibe. Each dispute opens into the full case for and against, with each side's actual evidence.

The labyrinth still exists intact under Petrie's stone bed (the "roof, not foundation" inversion).

For: De Cordier, the four scan missions, three ancient roof descriptions. Against: Petrie's verdict, quarry evidence, a century of orthodoxy.

Full case, both sides
The case for

Three eyewitness-era authors independently describe a stone roof: Herodotus ("a roof made of stone like the walls"), Diodorus ("a roof made of a single stone"), Strabo (single-stone roofs, and a walkable "plain of masonry" on top — which is what a buried roof would look like from above). The Mataha scans report a granite-like grid at 8–12 m below Petrie's slab, on both sides of the canal; Brown's GPR found the entrance shaft and the pyramid's true corner deep underground. Johanna's video (SRC_4) adds that the grid sits 20–25° off the pyramid's orientation — read as a hint the structure predates the pyramid. SRC_1 · 4 · 10 · 16

The case against

Petrie found a six-foot stratum of limestone chips over the uniform beton bed — physical demolition debris — and Pliny records the site serving as the district's quarry for centuries, with stone still being carted off for a railway in the 19th century. A quarried-away building explains the chips; an intact roof doesn't. Eric Uphill's comparative reconstruction (laid out in the last dispute below) needs no hidden levels at all: a standard 12th-Dynasty mortuary complex whose sprawl merely felt maze-like to Greek visitors. And the scholarly source's bottom line: with conflicting texts and minimal remains, no reliable reconstruction is currently possible — which cuts against both camps' confidence. Nobody in the pile reconciles the chip stratum with the intact-roof theory. SRC_6 · 11 · 16

SRC_1 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 10 · 16

The case against

Egypt's standing rule is that the ministry controls announcements of finds, and the Rogan guest — a believer — offers that reading himself: "I kind of don't really blame him… this was a political decision." The strongest counter is current events: an Egyptian-led excavation opened at Hawara in May 2026, which the same sources now celebrate. And the threat claim itself has exactly one source: the man telling his own story. SRC_1 · 15 · 16

A 40 m metallic object sits at the center of a four-level complex.

Sole origin: Akers's scan reading, NDA'd a decade, released after his death.

Full case, both sides
The case for

Akers was a real expert — ex-RAF, career in satellite detection of submarines and underground bunkers — using SAR technology his company sells commercially to oil and mineral clients, claiming kilometers of proven depth. His reading: four levels at 40/60/80/100 m, a central gallery at least 40×100 m, and a freestanding ~40 m object whose return signature was metallic — "unlike any he had detected in his entire career." unverifiable

Herodotus was reliable about the labyrinth.

Long treated as his "rare instance of unreliability"; mid-tier sources now read 2008 as vindication.

Full case, both sides
The case for

He claims eyewitness status plainly — "This I saw myself, and I found it greater than words can say" — and honestly separates what he saw (the upper chambers, walked room by room) from what he was told (the lower ones, which the priests refused). His geography checks out: Lake Moeris, the City of Crocodiles, the pyramid at the labyrinth's corner — all matching Hawara. The 2008 grid, if real, sits exactly where his account puts the underground half. SRC_8 · 11 · 12 · 16

The case against

The Egyptologist Alan Lloyd's analysis says only Herodotus and Strabo visited at all; the other four accounts are hearsay. The eyewitnesses disagree with each other — Herodotus's 12 courts vs. Strabo's courts-equal-nomes ("fewer than 30") vs. Pliny's 21 vs. Mela's 12 palaces and 1,000 houses — and Herodotus's key term (roughly "roofed courts") is a contradiction in Greek that scholars still argue over. He's also a historian "often accused" of over-schematising his numbers. Vindication claims overreach: one source asserts the radar found rooms "packed with magnificent hieroglyphs and murals," which radar cannot see. SRC_11 · 12

contested

The Hall of Records is at Hawara rather than Giza.

An intra-believer migration: Cayce said Giza; a century of searches found nothing; two sources relocate it.

Full case, both sides
The case for (as the believers argue it)

Every Giza search failed — SRI's 1978 drilling, the 1996 Schor survey, the 1999 water-shaft excavation — so maybe the site was wrong, not the archive. The labyrinth reads like a vault: Diodorus says it held "memorials of the homeland of each of the kings," the priests said the builders were older kings, and the Why Files makes the security argument outright — you don't hide your most precious secrets under the most famous landmark on Earth; you build a maze for them. SRC_2 · 3 · 4 · 7

The case against

The Hall of Records isn't ancient at all — the term starts with Edgar Cayce in the 1930s, drawing on Randall-Stevens and Spencer Lewis, who drew on Masonic and medieval legends. No classical author calls the labyrinth an archive of a lost civilization; Manetho flatly calls it a tomb. And the relocation itself is the tell: Wikipedia's history (via Colavito) documents exactly this pattern — when a predicted discovery fails to materialize, the belief migrates to fresher ground rather than dying. Hawara is the migration, arriving with zero new evidence. SRC_7 · 11

belief migration

One giant building vs. an ordinary pyramid complex that felt like a maze.

Petrie reconstructed one vast structure; the Egyptologist Eric Uphill dissolves it into standard 12th-Dynasty components.

Full case, both sides
One giant building (Petrie)

The beton bed measures 304 × 244 m — Petrie noted all the temples of Karnak, Luxor, and part of western Thebes would fit inside it — and the chip stratum ends abruptly at the building's former limits, implying one continuous structure. The ancient awe reads the same way: a single marvel, not a neighborhood. SRC_11 · 16

A complex that felt like one (Uphill)

Eric Uphill was an Egyptologist who specialized in Middle Kingdom royal architecture; his study of Hawara compared it to the five other known 12th-Dynasty pyramid complexes. On that evidence he rebuilds the site not as one building but as the standard parts of a royal funerary complex: a boat dock and quay on the canal, a valley temple, a causeway lined with two facing rows of queens' pyramids (his candidate for Herodotus's "12 courts"), and a mortuary temple — with the 1,500 underground chambers explained as the shaft-tombs of the adjacent cemetery. On this reading the Greeks weren't lying, they were disoriented: unfamiliar architecture, over-schematised on the way home. It also quietly explains why the accounts conflict — they were describing different parts of a sprawl. SRC_11

SECTION 6

Details & Anecdotes

The texture bin — specifics that don't fit the files but are exactly what makes a script feel researched. This section exists so "small" material can't silently vanish during synthesis. Each item opens into its full context.
  • Petrie got physically stuck in a collapsing sand tunnel inside the pyramid — candle out, pinned at the shoulders — and freed himself by the light of a match a worker tossed down. Then he went back in. SRC_3
    Context

    1888: the pyramid is mud brick over a stone core, so Petrie's tunnels kept collapsing as he hunted for the entrance. After freeing himself he tunneled the south side, found the real door with steps down — and had to continue through chest-deep saline water, rats, and Amenemhat's trap system to reach the burial chamber. The chamber was an architectural masterpiece and completely empty — tomb robbers had cleaned it out thousands of years before he arrived. The Why Files stages this as an adventure scene; the underlying events are in Petrie's own journals.

  • The pyramid's defenses — three concealed 20-ton sliding trapdoors, dummy passages, blind shafts — were all found disengaged, doors open. Petrie couldn't explain it; Wikipedia still can't. SRC_6
    Context

    The design was serious: each trapdoor opened onto a passage at a right angle, with mud-and-stone false blockings placed to waste robbers' time under threat of discovery. Wikipedia offers three explanations for why none of it was armed — burial-party negligence, an intention to return with more burials (the monolith held two sarcophagi with room for at least two more), or deliberate facilitation of robbery — and concludes "we cannot know." For a story about hidden chambers, the confirmed real mystery in the building is this one.

  • The burial chamber is a single 110-ton quartzite monolith. Inside: a lapis bead, burned bone, charcoal — robbers burned the pharaoh's mummy. SRC_6
    Context

    The monolith was lowered into a limestone-lined chamber, roofed with three 45-ton quartzite slabs, two relieving chambers, 50-ton limestone slabs in a pointed roof, and a three-foot brick arch — engineering entirely real and entirely orthodox. The burn evidence means the robbery was ancient and thorough: the wooden coffins and the king's body itself were used as fuel, probably for light or to melt off gilding.

  • Petrie's Hawara digs also produced the Fayum mummy portraits and a papyrus of the Iliad ("the Hawara Homer") — the site is a legitimate archaeological treasure independent of any labyrinth claim. SRC_6
    Context

    146 painted portraits from Roman-period coffins, among the only surviving panel paintings from classical antiquity; the Homer scroll (books 1–2 of the Iliad) now sits in the Bodleian. Useful framing: whatever is or isn't under the slab, Hawara already changed art history once — which is why "the site is worthless rubble" and "the site is priceless" are both wrong as framings.

  • Herodotus's underground informants weren't hiding technology, per the priests: the lower chambers held dead kings and sacred crocodiles. SRC_11 · 16
    Context

    His exact report: the lower 1,500 chambers were off-limits because they held "the sepulchres of the kings who had first built this labyrinth and of the sacred crocodiles." The crocodile detail is well grounded — the Fayum was crocodile-cult country (Crocodilopolis next door; Sobekneferu, who also built at Hawara, is named for Sobek), and Petrie excavated crocodile tomb-chapels northeast of the pyramid. The believer reading leans on "kings who had first built" as implying older builders; the mundane reading is that the restricted area was a royal-and-sacred necropolis, exactly what priests would restrict.
