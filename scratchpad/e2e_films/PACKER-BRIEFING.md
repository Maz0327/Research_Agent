# The full story behind a prospector's horrifying 1874 ordeal in Colorado's San Juan Mountains — what really happened, beyond what this video tells

## 1. The Read

Read all eleven entries. Here’s what is actually in the pile, told straight. The sources repeat one core story, but they do not provide eleven independent verdicts. The pile contains one narrative video, one archival collection page, several institutional and local-history accounts, a long Wikipedia entry, a forensic news report, and two magazine-style investigations. Nearly all repeat the same account: in February 1874, Alferd—also recorded as Alfred—Packer led five men into the snowbound San Juan Mountains, emerged alone two months later with their belongings and enough money to attract suspicion, admitted eating human flesh, and spent the rest of his life insisting that he killed only Shannon Bell in self-defense. The Wikipedia article repeats that account in grade-level versions, so those repetitions are not additional evidence. The sources offer different emphases: SRC\_1 is the fullest chronology; the Colorado State Archives page points to surviving confessions, court files, parole records, governor’s papers, and Supreme Court case numbers; James Starrs supplies the strongest forensic prosecution.

**Expedition:** Packer’s disaster had begun before the cannibalism. In late 1873, roughly twenty prospectors—accounts vary between twenty and twenty-one—left Utah for reported Colorado goldfields. Robert McGrue, leader of the larger prospecting party, led the larger party; Packer joined after the journey began, presenting himself as a guide familiar with the route. Born January 21, 1842, near Pittsburgh, Packer had served briefly in the Union Army before epilepsy caused two honorable discharges, and had worked as a hunter, teamster, ranch hand, miner, and guide. The accounts describe him as badly suited to guiding: he had little experience, got lost, quarreled, lied, stole, and fixated on how much money other men carried. Heavy snow slowed the expedition, provisions ran out, and by January 21, 1874, the prospectors reached Chief Ouray’s Ute encampment near Montrose already eating horse feed and considering the horses themselves. Ouray supplied food and shelter, offered to let them remain until spring, and warned that no Ute would cross the mountains in those conditions. Accounts differ over whether he said they would die or gave directions around the mountains. Most stayed with Ouray or took the safer Gunnison River route, although that party also nearly starved and needed cowhands’ help. Gold fever led six men onward.

**The crossing:** On February 9, Packer, Israel Swan, George Noon, Frank Miller, James Humphrey, a prospector who left Ouray’s camp, and Shannon Wilson Bell left Ouray’s camp. Sources differ over whether their provisions were meant to last seven or fourteen days, but all agree they were inadequate. They had little warm clothing, no snowshoes, few matches or no flint, and only a few rifles, a pistol, knives, and a hatchet. Ouray advised following the river; Packer argued that a direct mountain route was faster and led the group upward. The expedition leader, Robert McGrue, took them along the river until the horses could go no farther, unloaded their provisions, and turned back, knowing they lacked enough supplies. Packer later said that within days they had eaten their food, rawhide moccasins, rosebuds, and pine gum. He described cold, hunger, snow blindness, prayer, crying, and a vanished trail. After nine weeks, or sixty-five days, he reached the Los Piños Agency on April 16 carrying a rifle, knife, coffee pot, and other equipment, with live coals in the pot in one account—yet looking far better fed than his story suggested.

**Suspicion and confession:** At the agency Packer vomited when first given food. In Saguache he bought a $70 horse, spent roughly $100 at Dolan’s saloon on drinking, gambling, or poker, spent another $78 at a general store, and appeared to possess several wallets despite arriving supposedly penniless. The rifle belonged to an older missing prospector; a knife or pipe belonged to other party members. Preston Nutter, a prospector who rejected Packer’s account, and Oliver Loutsenhizer, men who had stayed with Ouray, rejected Packer’s claim that the others had abandoned him. The discovery of human flesh near the agency caused him to panic, faint, beg for mercy, and provide his first official confession, transcribed by clerk Herman Lauter. Packer gave three major confessions: the May 1874 statement, the March 1883 statement after his capture, and a final account sent in an August 1897 letter to the Rocky Mountain News. In the first, Swan died of hunger and exposure, Humphrey died days later, Miller was killed while Packer gathered wood, Bell shot Noon, and Packer killed Bell; he took money and flesh. In the 1883 version, Bell killed Miller, Humphrey, Noon, and possibly Swan while Packer was scouting, then attacked Packer with a hatchet. Packer said he shot Bell through the stomach, struck him in the head, and lived on the bodies for most of sixty days. His later letters kept that basic account: starvation made Bell violent, Packer killed him in self-defense, and cannibalism sustained him. He called the ordeal “the midnight horrors of starvation as well as those of utter isolation” and “the greatest wonder” that he returned to rationality.

**Bodies and escape:** In August 1874, John A. Randolph, an illustrator traveling for Harper’s Weekly, found all five bodies together in a pine-shaded gulch near the Lake Fork of the Gunnison, later called Deadman’s Gulch, and sketched the scene for the October 17 Harper’s Weekly issue. A twenty-person recovery party found severe violence: skull injuries consistent with blows, bodies stripped at meaty locations, deliberate removal of flesh, and cloth replacing shoes, suggesting the leather had been eaten. A path led to Packer’s crude shelter, where possessions remained. The remains contradicted his account of men dying one by one in scattered places, though they did not establish the exact sequence. Contemporary observers theorized that Packer killed the men for their belongings, became trapped by the storm, and used their bodies as a “natural refrigerator.” Before the charges could be upgraded to first-degree murder, Packer escaped the Saguache log-cabin jail with a key, penknife, and food; who helped him remains unknown. He hid for nine years under the name John Schwartze or Swartze. Jean “Frenchy” Cabazon recognized him in Cheyenne, Wyoming, on March 11, 1883, reported him, and helped return him for trial.

**1. Murder for money versus killing in extremity.:** The prosecution argued that Packer falsely presented himself as a guide, led the men onto a dangerous route, watched their finances, killed them for money, and invented a survival story. Swan was reportedly carrying cash—his family later said about $6,000—and a valuable Winchester. Packer’s contradictory accounts, spending spree, stolen-looking possessions, and attempted attack on agency clerk Herman Lauter damaged him further. At the second trial, hunters testified that deer, antelope, rabbits, and other game remained in the area, undermining his claim that starvation made cannibalism unavoidable. Packer’s narrower defense, later championed by Polly Pry and museum curator David Bailey, was that the men died or were killed by Bell, Packer shot Bell only to save himself, and he ate the dead because otherwise he would have died. Starrs’s modern middle position is that Packer probably killed the men for their belongings but resorted to cannibalism afterward out of necessity.

**2. What the bones prove.:** On July 17, 1989, James Starrs’s team exhumed the five men, who had been buried only about thirteen inches below ground. Starrs declared Packer “guilty as sin,” reporting blunt-force injuries, knife or defleshing marks, and a winter “natural refrigerator”; UPI said four men had been killed by hatchet blows and the fifth skull was missing. Walter Birkby, the forensic anthropologist who disputed Starrs, the forensic anthropologist assisting him, disagreed: “We found nothing to corroborate or refute anything Packer said. We’ll never know who did it based on any solid physical evidence.” The fuller account records defensive hacking marks on the forearms of three skeletons, including Bell, and possible bullet holes in pelvic bones, but no definitive conclusion. The evidence supports violence and postmortem butchering more firmly than it proves who attacked whom. It does not show that Packer fired the weapon, that the gun was his, or that the shooting was self-defense. In 1994, David Bailey found an 1862 Colt revolver with three .38-caliber rounds loaded and an accession note saying it came from the crime site. A lead fragment beneath Bell’s remains chemically matched the bullets, and an elk-hip test produced a similar hole. That is the strongest evidence for Packer’s claim that Bell was shot. Grant Houston and Michelle Pierce question the gun’s provenance because visitors routinely found old firearms and bullets near the site. The men were reburied after the exhumation.

**Trials, parole, and death:** Packer’s first trial in 1883 ended in a murder conviction and death sentence. The conviction was overturned because applying the later murder law to the 1874 territorial crime violated the applicable legal rules; the Colorado Supreme Court reversed the sentence in October 1885. His second trial, moved to Gunnison amid local prejudice, ended June 8, 1886, in five voluntary-manslaughter convictions and a forty-year sentence, eight years per victim—the longest U.S. prison sentence then reported. The famous political insult about Packer eating “five of the seven Democrats” is apocryphal or later retelling; the surviving court record gives a conventional judgment ordering him hanged “until you are dead, dead, dead.” Packer filed five unsuccessful appeals; the Colorado Supreme Court upheld the sentence June 19, 1899. Governor Charles Thomas paroled—not pardoned—him on February 8, 1901, after he had served eighteen years, on the condition that he not profit from his story. Sources otherwise vary between fifteen and eighteen years and between 1901 and 1905. Packer worked as a guard, ranch hand, or laborer, made horsehair bridles and dollhouses in prison, returned to prospecting, and was later described as modest and charitable. He died April 23, 1907, aged sixty-five, in Jefferson County, Colorado.

**Legacy and limits:** Polly Pry, whose real name was Leonel Campbell Ross O’Bryan and who became the Denver Post’s first female reporter, portrayed Packer as a Civil War veteran and victim of a prejudiced system. Her campaign, aided by petitions and supporters including Duane Hatch, helped secure parole, not a pardon; the State Archives preserves the records that could test the campaign in detail. Packer’s letters maintained that he killed only Bell, while women including Rebecca Newman wrote seeking his account and sympathy. After release, some Lake City residents saw a calculating killer; others saw a damaged survivor. Popular culture chose the joke: Alferd Packer restaurants and grills, “Have a friend for lunch,” coffin races, mock trials, a Packer burger, Cannibal! The Musical, and other lore. The strongest sources establish that five men died violently, Packer ate them, he was convicted of manslaughter, and deliberate killing is plausible. They do not definitively reconstruct the killings: no eyewitness survives; the chain of custody, every skull and pelvic injury, and the revolver’s ownership remain unsettled; the Archives page lists confessions and court files without presenting them in full; and no source reconciles Starrs’s certainty with Birkby’s agnosticism. The case therefore answers neither exactly who killed whom nor why.

## 2. The Players

**Alfred Griner Packer** — Los Piños Indian Agency arrival - source of the first account of the mountain journey

Also known as the Colorado Cannibal, Alfred arrived at the agency in rags, carrying a rifle and a coffee pot, and told agency men that the others had abandoned him after exhaustion, snow blindness, and frostbite. He said he survived for months on rabbits, roots, and rosebuds, drawing on his experience as a guide. General Charles Adams recorded Alfred’s confession, investigated the missing men, and took him to trial. Alfred recurred because his changing accounts, the belongings he carried, and his survival became the center of the cannibalism and murder story.

**James Packer** — Prospector and confessed participant in the San Juan Mountains cannibalism story

Packer was part of a band of twenty gold seekers who left Utah and later split into two groups. He said the men survived after their provisions ran out by cooking raw-hide moccasins and eating wild rose buds, and he reached the Los Piños Indian Agency nine weeks after leaving Utah with a Winchester rifle, a skinning knife, and a coffee pot containing live coals. He later played high-stakes poker, bought a horse, and was questioned about his money. His confession became one of the West’s enduring legends and murder mysteries.

**Shannon Wilson Bell** — Prospector identified as a victim and, in Packer’s account, the other armed survivor

Packer said Bell killed James Humphrey, George Noon, and Frank Miller while they slept, then attacked Packer with a hatchet when Packer returned from searching for food. Packer said he shot Bell twice in self-defense and then lived largely on the dead men’s flesh. A body was identified as Bell, and the bullet match suggested that Packer did shoot him in self-defense. Bell’s pipe was among the objects used to connect Packer to the missing party.

**Leonel Campbell Ross O’Bryan** — Reporter who wrote under the name Polly Pry and campaigned for Packer’s release

O’Bryan, born in 1857 and dead on 16 July 1938, was a controversial Denver Post reporter who later worked as a freelancer. Writing as Polly Pry, O’Bryan visited Packer in prison in 1899, portrayed him as a common man and victim of circumstances, and launched a media campaign around his plight. Packer respected Pry for the rest of his life and called her his Liberator. Many believed Pry and her employers’ efforts helped secure Packer’s release and his later job at The Denver Post.

**Chief Ouray** — Ute leader whose winter camp offered the prospectors shelter and a safer route

On January 21, 1874, the party reached Chief Ouray’s camp near Montrose in the Uncompahgre Valley. Ouray supplied food and lodging, warned that traveling in the winter conditions meant certain death, and urged the men to stay until spring. He advised them to follow the Gunnison River before heading north to the agency. The prospectors ignored his warning and left on February 9 with insufficient food, making Ouray’s offer and advice a recurring contrast to their later ordeal.

**Israel Swan** — Elderly prospector whose death and possessions anchor competing versions of Packer’s confession

Packer said Swan died from hunger and exposure after giving him a rifle, while his signed first confession said Old Man Swan died first and was eaten by the other five people. In another account, Packer returned from gathering firewood to find Swan killed by a hatchet, after which the men ate parts of his body and divided several thousand dollars found on him. Packer and Bell later carried Swan’s rifle and money. Swan was one of five men seeking to reach Breckenridge before the arrival of thousands of fortune hunters.

**General Charles Adams** — Los Piños Indian Agency official who recorded Packer’s confession and brought him to trial

Adams oversaw the agency, had his men feed Alfred, and sent a mounted man to bring him in for questioning. He asked about Packer’s money, the other men’s belongings, and how he had survived while appearing well-fed, then had the confession written down and signed as a sworn statement. Adams later led a search for the bodies, found nothing, confirmed Packer’s identity in Denver, and took him away for trial. He also questioned Packer again about what had happened.

**James E. Starrs** — Forensic law professor who led the 1989 exhumation of the five bodies

Starrs, a George Washington University professor specializing in forensic science, led the exhumation of the five bodies 115 years after the deaths. With Walter H. Birkby, he said cannibalism could not be scientifically demonstrated without an eyewitness, but he concluded that the evidence showed Packer had murdered his comrades. Starrs believed Packer likely killed them for their belongings and resorted to cannibalism out of necessity. His conclusions recur because they supplied a later forensic interpretation of the disputed story.

**Frank Miller** — Prospector identified in Packer’s account as a selected and killed member of the party

Miller was one of five men seeking to reach Breckenridge before thousands of fortune hunters arrived. Packer said he quarreled constantly with Miller and that Bell, Humphrey, Noon, and he secretly decided Miller would be next because he was stocky and had a lot of soft flesh. Packer said Miller was killed with a hatchet blow to the head while stooping to gather firewood. Miller’s death became part of the account used to explain the missing party’s bodies.

**Preston Nutter** — Leader of the river-following party and early accuser of Packer

Nutter arrived at the agency in May and led the other party that had followed the river. He later found Packer in Dolan’s Saloon, questioned him about the missing men and their belongings, fought with him, and described him as sulky, obstinate, quarrelsome, and a petty thief. Nutter doubted that five miners unfamiliar with Colorado would abandon their guide and questioned Packer’s account of Swan’s rifle and Miller’s knife. He reported Packer’s spending and possessions to Adams and later identified the five bodies.

**David P. Bailey** — Museum of Western Colorado curator - investigator of Packer’s shooting claim

In 1994, Bailey began a ten-year investigation into whether Packer had shot Shannon Bell in self-defense. He found an 1862 Colt revolver while inventorying historic firearms, discovered archived forensic samples from the 1989 exhumation, and arranged for Richard Dujay and Mesa State College’s Electron Microscopy Lab to examine them. The lead fragment’s chemical composition matched bullets from Bailey’s revolver, leading Bailey to believe he had solved the case, although by 2000 he had not proved the pistol’s link to Packer and Michelle Pierce remained unconvinced. Bailey recurs because his forensic evidence became part of the later debate and was used in mock trials, where its inclusion led jurors to acquit Packer more often than not.

**James Humphrey** — Packer expedition member - alleged victim in the competing accounts of the killings

Packer said Shannon Bell killed Humphrey, George Noon, and Frank Miller as they slept, and that he, Bell, Humphrey, and Noon had secretly decided Miller would be next. In Packer’s signed confession, Humphrey died four or five days later, was eaten, and had about $133, whose pocketbook and money Packer said he took; an amended version instead attributed Humphrey’s death to exposure. Humphrey’s exhumed remains, found with Noon’s, showed a mostly flayed torso attached to skeletal legs, a decayed bearded face, suspected hatchet blows, and broken bones. He recurs because his death and remains are central to the conflicting accounts of murder, exposure, and survival cannibalism.

**Tom Miller** — Packer bust unveiling sculptor - subject of a disputed attribution involving Lamm

Packer said Miller was selected because he was stocky and had a lot of soft flesh, then killed by a hatchet blow while stooping to pick up firewood; Packer also said Miller had left a knife stuck in a tree. Separately, the New York Times reported in 1982 that Lamm congratulated sculptor Tom Miller at the unveiling of Packer’s bust, where Lamm quoted Packer as saying, “Well done!” Miller recurs because the name appears both in Packer’s account of the expedition and in the later bust-unveiling report.

**George Noon** — Packer expedition member - alleged victim whose exhumed remains were examined

Packer said Shannon Bell killed Noon, James Humphrey, and Frank Miller as they slept, while he, Bell, Humphrey, and Noon had secretly decided Miller would be next. Noon’s remains, found with Humphrey’s, were mostly a flayed torso attached to skeletal legs; his bearded face remained intact, and the body showed suspected hatchet blows and broken bones. The remains still contained substantial flesh, muscle, and organs that could have been eaten before Bell allegedly tried to murder Packer. Noon recurs because his death and remains figure in both Packer’s survival account and the forensic interpretation of the bodies.

**M. B. Gerry** — District Court judge - presiding judge at Packer’s first trial and sentencing

Judge Melville Gerry ordered the sheriff to search the highways and byways for additional jurors. The jury found Packer guilty of murder, and Gerry sentenced him to be “hung from your neck until you are dead, dead, dead.” Newspapers attributed more colorful insults to Gerry, including the claim that Packer had eaten five of seven Democrats in Hinsdale County, but the material presents that famous quotation as likely false. Fred M. Mazzula used trial transcripts to identify Gerry’s less colorful but accurate words, so Gerry recurs both as the sentencing judge and as the source of a disputed quotation.

**Charles Thomas** — Colorado governor - granted Packer parole in 1901 without a pardon

Petitions influenced by Pry’s stories were sent to Governor Charles Thomas, and supporters argued that extreme privation might have impaired Packer’s responsibility. Thomas ultimately relented and, as his last official act before leaving office, paroled Packer in 1901 but did not pardon him. The parole was conditional on Packer not trying to profit from his story. Thomas recurs because he was the official who converted the campaign for Packer’s release into parole rather than exoneration.

**Rebecca A. Newman** — Packer sympathizer and correspondent - requested his detailed account from prison

Newman, born in 1846 in Herkimer, New York, worked as a schoolteacher in the Bronx and opened communications with Packer to express sympathy and apparently seek a friendship. After Governor-Elect Alva Adams warned her against defending or excusing Packer, she persisted; in 1887 Adams forwarded her letter to the State Penitentiary. Newman asked for a full account of Packer’s trials and sufferings, and Packer repeated his self-defense and survival-cannibalism story, tried to shift blame onto Ouray, and described his lack of arrest or censure before 1883. She recurs because her correspondence gave Packer a channel for presenting his own account, while the text also says he had a hidden intent to see what she could do for him.

**Herman Lauter** — Constable and search-party leader - target of Packer’s alleged knife attack

Lauter transcribed Packer’s first official statement and led a search party made up of five Utah miners, agency officers, and Packer as guide. On the return journey, Packer allegedly tried to murder Lauter with a knife; he was caught, restrained, and arrested for the attack. As constable, Lauter testified that Packer lunged at him with murder in his eyes after being ordered to surrender the knife. Lauter recurs because he connects Packer’s first official statement, the search for the bodies, and the arrest that preceded the case.

**John A. Randolph** — Harper’s Weekly illustrator - discoverer and reporter of the five bodies

While traveling over the mountains in August, Randolph came down a pass near Lake City and walked by a tributary of the Gunnison River near the spot where Packer said he had left Shannon. He noticed something protruding from the ground, smelled a terrible stench, and found five men in different stages of decomposition. Randolph sketched the scene and reported it to authorities in Lake City. He recurs because his discovery at the foot of Slumgullion Pass, two miles southeast of Lake City, brought the bodies and the incident to official attention.

**Oliver D. Loutsenhizer** — Expedition member and prosecution witness - critic of Packer’s account and qualifications

The party split into two groups, with five men following Loutsenhizer along the Gunnison River while six, including Packer, headed through the San Juan Mountains. Loutsenhizer threatened to shoot Packer if he followed the other group, later told Adams that Packer was not trustworthy, and identified the Winchester Packer carried as belonging to an elderly party member and a pipe left at the agency as Shannon Bell’s. He and Preston Nutter challenged Packer’s qualifications as a guide and emphasized his character flaws, inconsistent stories, and contradictions. Loutsenhizer testified for the prosecution alongside Otto Mears, Larry Dolan, and Nutter, who was the key witness.

**Robert McGrue** — Party organizer and river-route guide in the Packer expedition

McGrue assembled 20 people in Salt Lake City to travel to the San Juan Mountains after hearing of gold in Breckenridge. He guided Packer’s group along the river route advised by Chief Ouray until his horses could not continue, then unloaded the men’s provisions and returned to Ouray’s camp. Members of his party later said Packer had overstated or possibly fabricated his experience with the area. McGrue recurs as part of the original expedition network with Chief Ouray and Preston Nutter.

**Jean “Frenchy” Cabazon** — Packer’s discoverer and identity witness in Cheyenne

Cabazon, a member of the original party, had stayed in Chief Ouray’s camp before reaching the destination with Bob McGrue and Preston Nutter’s party. On March 11, 1883, he found Packer in Cheyenne, Wyoming, living under the alias John Schwartze, after Packer approached him to buy supplies. Cabazon reported him to the sheriff, confirmed his identity when summoned, and accompanied him by train to Denver for Packer’s second confession.

**Trey Parker** — Cannibal! The Musical director and South Park co-creator

Parker and CU film student Matt Stone produced Cannibal! The Musical in 1993, which was Parker’s directorial debut. The two later created South Park. Parker recurs because Cannibal! The Musical is tied to the cultural retelling of Packer’s story.

**Richard Lamm** — Colorado governor who declined Packer’s posthumous pardon

Lamm declined Packer’s posthumous pardon the year before a 1982 Capitol Rotunda bust unveiling. At the unveiling, he acknowledged criticism of that refusal, addressed claims that Packer killed only one man in self-defense, and made a joke about the evidence being “in the pudding.” He also congratulated sculptor Tom Miller and quoted Packer as saying, “Well done!”

**Michelle Pierce** — Former Lake City town clerk and archival critic of the official Packer account

Pierce believes Packer did not murder the five men. Her archival research found details that do not fit the official narrative, and she was unconvinced that Bailey’s pistol was the smoking gun. She portrays Packer as a pitiable soul whose social pathologies arose from a misunderstood disease and the harsh judgments of his contemporaries.

**Nick Charles** — Fictional detective who references Packer in The Thin Man

In Dashiell Hammett’s novel The Thin Man, Nick Charles advises Gilbert Wynant to read a selection about Packer’s expeditions. He recurs as a fictional literary reference to the Packer story.

**Grant Houston** — Hinsdale County Historical Society head and Lake City Silver World editor and publisher

Houston says many people in Lake City believe Packer was guilty and believed he lured the men into the mountains for their money or guns before killing them. He remained skeptical because tourists had long found old guns and bullets near the massacre site and claimed they belonged to Packer or the other prospectors. Houston recurs as a local historical voice reporting both the town’s prevailing belief and doubts about the physical evidence.

**Harry Heye Tammen** — Denver Post publisher who funded Packer’s defense and retrial effort

Tammen and fellow Denver Post publisher Fred Bonfils hired a lawyer to defend Packer. Pry persuaded Tammen to pay for Packer’s defense and demand a retrial. Tammen recurs through the Denver Post’s intervention in Packer’s legal case and the account of Pry shielding him from Anderson.

**Larry Dolan** — Saguache saloon owner who hosted Packer and later testified for the prosecution

Packer played high-stakes poker at Dolan’s saloon, bought a $70 horse, and booked a room there. Dolan later said Packer spent about $100 during his stay and offered to lend him $300. Preston Nutter later found Packer in the saloon, and Dolan testified for the prosecution alongside Otto Mears, Oliver D. Loutsenhizer, and Nutter, who was the key witness.

**Richard Dujay** — Mesa State College scientist who analyzed forensic samples from Shannon Bell’s remains

In 2001, Dujay analyzed archived samples with an electron microscope after Bailey arranged for him and the Electron Microscopy Lab at Mesa State College to examine them. Dujay’s team found microscopic lead fragments, including a tiny fragment of lead in soil taken from under Shannon Bell’s remains. He recurs because this analysis is part of the forensic examination of the Packer evidence.

**Walter H. Birkby** — Cannibalism evidence commentator

Walter H. Birkby, along with Starrs, said cannibalism could not be scientifically demonstrated unless someone actually saw the eating. He also said there was nothing to corroborate or refute Packer’s account and that no solid physical evidence could establish who did it. He recurs because his statements frame the limits of the evidence surrounding the case.

**Duane Hatch** — Parole campaigner for Packer

Duane Hatch led a campaign for Packer’s parole and petitioned for his release for nearly a decade. Polly Pry learned of the campaign. Hatch recurs as the person associated with the sustained effort to secure Packer’s release.

**Matt Stone** — CU film student and South Park co-creator

Matt Stone and Trey Parker, both CU film students, produced Cannibal! The Musical in 1993. They later created the television show South Park. Stone recurs because their work brought the Packer story into film and television comedy.

**Otto Mears** — General-store figure and Packer prosecution witness

Packer spent an additional $78 in Otto Mears’ general store. Mears testified for the prosecution, alongside Larry Dolan, Oliver D. Loutsenhizer, and Preston Nutter; Nutter was the key witness. Mears said Packer had valuable Wells Fargo drafts when he came out of the mountains, linking him both to Packer’s spending and to the prosecution’s evidence.

**Phil Ochs** — Songwriter of “The Ballad of Alferd Packer”

Phil Ochs wrote “The Ballad of Alferd Packer” in 1964 about the expedition and its aftermath. Its humor reflects a seemingly lighthearted ongoing attitude toward Packer and his alleged crimes. The song appeared on none of Ochs’s studio or live albums, but several compilations issued after his death in 1976 included it, most recently the 2010 compilation On My Way, drawn from 1963 demos. Ochs is dead.

**W. W. Anderson** — Attorney in the Pry-Tammen confrontation

During the confrontation, attorney W. W. Anderson pulled a gun and shot both men. As he prepared to shoot again, Pry moved in front of Harry Heye Tammen and tried to shield him, stalling Anderson until police arrived. The film included the incident, though Anderson was not named and was called [the supplied material ends here].

**Esther Packer** — Mother of Alferd Packer

Esther Packer, née Griner, was the mother of one of three children born to James Packer and her. She recurs in the family background identifying Packer’s parentage.

**Fred Bonfils** — Denver Post publisher and Packer defense sponsor

In 1898, Frederick Bonfils hired Pry for the Denver Post. Bonfils and fellow Denver Post publisher Harry Tammen also hired a lawyer to defend Packer. He recurs through both the newspaper connection to Pry and the legal defense of Packer.

**Thomas Samuel Duke** — Author of Celebrated Criminal Cases of America

Thomas Samuel Duke’s Celebrated Criminal Cases of America supplied a selection that gives Packer’s first name as Alfred. He recurs as a published source for the naming and presentation of the case.

**Barbara Walters** — ABC Evening News correspondent for the 1977 plaque event

Barbara Walters covered the 1977 plaque event for ABC Evening News. She recurs because the event entered national television coverage through her report.

**Fred M. Mazzula** — Al Packer, A Colorado Cannibal author and trial-transcript researcher

Mazzula used trial transcripts to determine what Judge Gerry actually said. In his book Al Packer, A Colorado Cannibal, he argued that Gerry did not speak like the exaggerated quote suggests. He recurs as a source for checking the wording attributed to Gerry.

**George Anthony** — Railroad industrialist who eloped to Mexico with Pry

When Pry was fifteen, she eloped with George Anthony to Mexico. Five years later, she left him and moved to New York to become a reporter. He recurs in accounts of Pry’s early life and marriage.

**Toddy Walters** — The Musical performer who played Polly Pry

Walters portrayed Polly Pry in The Musical. The production explored Pry’s involvement with the case more deeply but ignored that Pry was married, presenting her as a love interest for Packer. Walters recurs as part of the musical’s treatment of Pry.

**Alva Adams** — Governor-Elect who warned Newman about defending Packer

When Adams learned of Newman’s affection for Packer, he warned her against defending Packer or excusing his conduct. He recurs in the account of the pressure surrounding Newman’s relationship to Packer’s case.

**C. W. McCall** — Songwriter of “Comin’ Back for More” about Packer

McCall wrote the song “Comin’ Back for More” about Packer. He recurs as a figure in the musical and popular treatment of the Packer story.

**Cynthia Nessin** — Polly Pry portrayer in The Legend of Alfred Packer

Nessin portrayed Polly Pry in Jim Roberson’s 1980 film The Legend of Alfred Packer. She recurs as part of that film’s depiction of Pry.

**Dashiell Hammett** — The Thin Man novelist who referenced Packer’s expeditions

## 3. The Organisations

**The Denver Post** — Denver newspaper that employed Polly Pry and campaigned for Alfred Packer’s release

The Denver Post employed Polly Pry, who visited Packer in prison in 1899 and wrote about him as a common man and victim of circumstances. A Denver Post columnist crusaded for Packer’s release, and the paper later petitioned for it. After parole in 1901, Packer worked for a time as a guard at the paper, a job many believed resulted from Pry and the paper securing his release.

**Colorado State Archives** — Holder of legal and historical records concerning Alfred Packer

The Colorado State Archives holds several legal and historical records concerning Packer. **Colorado Supreme Court** — State court that overturned and later upheld parts of Alfred Packer’s case

The Colorado Supreme Court granted a change of venue for Packer’s second trial in Gunnison. In October 1885, it reversed his death sentence on ex post facto grounds because the crime occurred before statehood and a territorial statute barred the death penalty. On June 19, 1899, it officially upheld his sentence; the collection includes several related case numbers from 1883 through 1898.

## 4. The Places

**Los Piños Indian Agency** — remote Ute agency near Saguache on the Gunnison River—the party’s intended outpost

On April 16, 1874, Alfred Packer arrived alone during a snowstorm after the party’s river journey had run out of food. He told agency men about the expedition, but his healthy appearance, unexplained money, and account of the missing men made the agency central to the investigation.

**Lake City** — Colorado town near the Slumgullion Pass discovery site and the first trial venue

Randolph found all five bodies in a gulch two miles southeast of Lake City and reported the discovery to authorities there. Because the alleged crime was considered to have occurred in Hinsdale County, Packer was detained and tried in Lake City, where he and his counsel argued that local prejudice made a fair trial impossible; the town later became a center of Packer attractions and commemorations.

**Saguache** — Colorado town where Packer stayed after leaving Los Piños Indian Agency

After recovering at the agency, Packer went to Saguache, rented a room and a saloon, bought a horse, and spent more than $100 drinking and gambling. He was later arrested for manslaughter and held in the sheriff’s log cabin, while witnesses described his behavior and changing accounts of what had happened.

**Denver** — Colorado city where Packer was taken for identification and a second confession

General Adams traveled to Denver to confirm Packer’s identity and take him away for trial, and Cabazon later accompanied him there by train for a second confession. Packer also moved to Denver before settling in Littleton because he found city life intolerable and wanted to be nearer the mountains.

**Gunnison** — Colorado town where Packer’s retrial and final conviction took place

After the Colorado Supreme Court granted a change of venue, Packer’s second trial was moved to Gunnison, where he had been incarcerated for nearly four years. In 1886 he was convicted of manslaughter and sentenced to 40 years, becoming a local celebrity while awaiting retrial and during the proceedings.

**San Juan Mountains** — Colorado mountain region crossed by Packer and five prospectors in winter 1874

Six men entered the mountains hoping to reach goldfields near Breckenridge, but the route became snowbound and Packer led his group higher despite Chief Ouray’s warning. Packer later confessed that the party had starved, that Israel Swan had died from hunger and exposure, and that he had eaten him; the material also places the victims’ mass grave in the San Juan Mountains, while another account places the bodies near Lake City.

**Hinsdale County** — Colorado county containing Lake City, where the alleged crime was prosecuted

Packer was returned to Hinsdale County after his arrest in Wyoming and was tried there after the authorities determined that the crime had occurred within the county rather than Saguache County. He was found guilty of murder and sentenced to hang, although the proceedings ultimately led to his detention and prosecution in Lake City.

**Breckenridge** — Colorado goldfield destination that drew the Utah prospectors

The travelers intended to reach newly found goldfields near Breckenridge after stopping at Los Piños Indian Agency. Packer claimed he knew the route and had been hired to guide the five men there, making the destination part of the story’s question about how he joined the party.

**Boulder** — Colorado city associated with later cultural portrayals of Alfred Packer

The Alferd Packer Grill was named in Packer’s honor, and residents and UC-Boulder students have held mock trials and Al Packer Days. The comedy musical Cannibal! The Musical was released in Boulder in 1993 before its wider U.S. release in 1996, making Boulder a recurring site of Packer’s popular afterlife.

**Lake Fork of the Gunnison River** — River route and site above which Packer’s companions’ bodies were found

After two weeks, Packer said he was lost near the Lake Fork and did not recognize the area. Five prospectors followed him southeast and then south along the river on February 9, and the bodies later lay above it in a place now called Dead Man’s Gulch.

**Gunnison River** — River route toward the Los Pinós Indian Agency

Chief Ouray directed the party to descend to the Gunnison River, follow it, and then head north to the agency. The travelers split, with five men following Oliver Loutsenhizer along the river; two reached the agency and three arrived a few days later, while most historians place the discovery of the remains near the river.

**Wyoming** — State where Packer was apprehended after leaving Colorado

Sources differ on the timing, saying either that Packer was captured nine years later or apprehended in 1883. He was arrested in Wyoming under another identity and returned to Lake City or Hinsdale County for trial.

**Cheyenne** — Wyoming city where Packer was found living under an alias

On March 11, 1883, Jean “Frenchy” Cabazon found Packer in Cheyenne living as “John Schwartze.” Cabazon confirmed his identity and accompanied him by train to Denver, where Packer made his second confession.

**Colorado Territory** — Territory containing the San Juan Mountains and the party’s intended mining region

Packer emerged as the sole survivor of a six-man party traveling through the San Juan Mountains during the winter of 1874, after guiding five men into the territory during a snowstorm and committing cannibalism. Before statehood, Colorado Territory had no murder statute under which he could be tried, a legal circumstance that shaped the case.

**Littleton** — Colorado suburb of Denver where Packer later lived and was buried

Packer moved to Littleton because he found city life intolerable and wanted to be closer to the mountains; there he bought a copper mine and returned to prospecting. He died there in 1907 and was buried in Littleton, whose grave and memorial remain part of the story.

**Bingham Canyon** — Utah mining area from which the prospecting party set out for the San Juan region

Packer joined or led a prospecting party that departed Bingham Canyon in November 1873, after telling five men and 15 other miners that he could guide them to Colorado’s rich precious-metal region. The sources disagree on the departure date, giving November 8 and November 20.

**Jefferson County** — Colorado county identified in accounts of Packer’s death

Packer’s death is placed in Jefferson County in 1907, with one account specifying a ranch near Deer Creek in western Jefferson County. The location conflicts with accounts that place his death in Littleton, and the sources also disagree about his age, giving 65 and 69.

**Montrose** — location near present-day Montrose, Colorado - site of Chief Ouray’s Ute camp

On January 21, 1874, the exhausted party reached a Ute encampment led by Chief Ouray in the Uncompahgre Valley just outside Montrose. The place remains central because it marks the party’s encounter with the Utes after Alfred had guided them there only by luck.

**Cañon City** — Colorado State Penitentiary in Cañon City - site of Packer’s imprisonment

After the 1886 retrial, Packer was convicted of manslaughter and sentenced to forty years of hard labor at the penitentiary. The story returns to Cañon City because Packer served his sentence there and was visited by Denver Post writer Polly Pry in 1899.

**Deadman’s Gulch** — gulch five miles beyond town, above the Lake Fork of the Gunnison River - site where the bodies were found

The bodies were found at this site, which is described as lying above the Lake Fork of the Gunnison River. The place remains tied to the case through its name, now also rendered as Dead Man’s Gulch.

**Ottumwa** — Ottumwa, Iowa - place where Packer enlisted in the 8th Iowa Cavalry Regiment

Packer enlisted there on June 25, 1863. Ottumwa returns to the story as part of his Union military history before the later events involving his party.

**San Antonio** — Ripley’s Museum in San Antonio, Texas - reported location of the skull as of 2008

As of 2008, the skull was reported to be at Ripley’s Museum in San Antonio after being relocated from New Orleans following Hurricane Katrina. The city remains relevant because it was the reported repository of this physical evidence.

**Winona** — Winona, Minnesota - place where Packer enlisted in the Union army

Packer enlisted there on April 22, 1862, and was assigned to Company F of the 16th Infantry Regiment. Winona returns to the story as an early point in his military record.

**Cannibal Plateau** — area northeast of Lake City - location where Packer’s party got lost

Packer’s party became lost in this area, which is labeled “Cannibal Plateau” on maps. In the 1950s, a rusted 1862 Colt Police Model .32 five-shot revolver was found there with two shots missing.

**Salt Lake City** — city where Robert McGrue assembled the party for the San Juan Mountains journey

Robert McGrue assembled a party of twenty there after hearing of gold discovered in Breckenridge, and the group later met Alfred about twenty-five miles south of the city. Salt Lake City also recurs because rumors claimed Packer had been jailed there for allegedly murdering a hunting partner.

**Slumgullion Pass** — pass two miles southeast of Lake City, Colorado, at the foot of which the bodies were found

Randolph discovered all five bodies in a pine-shaded gulch at the foot of Slumgullion Pass. Alfred took the pass's yellow color and stew-like name as evidence that he was there, making it central to both the discovery and his account.

**Douglas** — town in Wyoming where Packer was found in March 1883

Packer was found there living under the alias John Swartze. Douglas returns to the story because the discovery exposed where he was after the deaths and connected him to a false identity.

**New Orleans** — former location of the skull before its reported relocation to San Antonio

The skull was reported to have been relocated from New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina. The city returns to the story because it was part of the skull's reported chain of custody before it was said to be at Ripley's Museum in San Antonio in 2008.

**Pennsylvania** — state where Alfred Griner Packer was born and where he intended to return

Packer was born in Allegheny County, near Pittsburgh, on January 21, 1842. After staying at the agency for ten days, he went to Saguache to buy supplies for his trip back home to Pennsylvania, making the state both part of his background and his stated destination.

**Provo** — Utah location near which the party met the young Alfred Packer

George Tracy said the party met the 23-year-old Packer near Provo. The original party later left Provo, with Cabazon among its members, so the place marks the party's starting point and Packer's entry into the account.

**Uncompahgre Valley** — valley near Montrose, Colorado, where the party reached Chief Ouray's camp

On January 21, 1874, the party came to Chief Ouray's camp there. The valley returns to the story because the camp was a stopping point before the party continued toward its destination.

**Utah Territory** — territory where Packer recruited miners by claiming knowledge of the route to Colorado

Before the deaths of his five companions, Packer told the men and 15 other miners near Bingham Canyon that he could guide them to Colorado Territory's rich precious-metal region. He also claimed to know the territory between Utah and Brackenridge well, making Utah Territory the setting for the deception that launched the journey.

**Ute Reservation** — the Ute reservation, where Packer said the crime occurred in early 1874

Packer appealed for a retrial, arguing that because the crime occurred on the reservation, the case should have been under federal rather than local jurisdiction. The case also reflects prospectors’ eagerness to trespass into Ute territory.

**Allegheny County** — Allegheny County, Pennsylvania, near Pittsburgh - Packer’s birthplace

Alfred Griner Packer was born there on January 21, 1842. The county returns the story to Packer’s early life before his later travels and criminal case.

**Capitol Rotunda** — the Capitol Rotunda, where a bust of Packer was unveiled in 1982

At the unveiling, Governor Lamm discussed his refusal to grant Packer an unconditional pardon and addressed claims that Packer had killed only one man in self-defense. Lamm’s remark that “the proof was in the pudding and Alferd ate that, too” helped return the story to the disputed evidence and Packer’s contested reputation.

**Deer Creek** — a ranch near Deer Creek in western Jefferson County - Packer’s place of death

Packer died there two years after the events described in the case. His death keeps the location in the story as the endpoint of his life.

**LaGrange County** — LaGrange County, Indiana - where Packer’s family lived by the early 1850s

Packer’s father moved the family there by the early 1850s and worked as a cabinetmaker. The county marks part of Packer’s family history before his later travels.

**Lake San Cristobal** — a frozen lake the party may have reached after following a stream for a day

Alfred said the party followed the stream until they reached a frozen lake, possibly Lake San Cristobal. The uncertainty over the identification keeps the lake tied to the account without establishing it as the definite location.

**Mexico** — Mexico, where Pry eloped at age fifteen

Pry eloped there with the railroad industrialist George Anthony. The place is part of Pry’s early history and returns in the account as the setting for that elopement.

**Spanish Trail** — route the party was traveling

The party encountered heavy snow there, which hid the path and forced them to rely almost entirely on a compass. The trail returns to the story because the snow made it difficult to follow their route.

- **November 8, 1873** — Packer led a prospect party of 21 men that departed Bingham Canyon, Utah, on November 8, 1873, hoping to be among the first to locate mining claims in the San Juan region. (SRC_10)

- **November 1873** — Before the deaths of his five companions, Packer lied in November 1873 to those men and 15 other miners near Bingham Canyon, Utah Territory, saying he could guide them to Colorado Territory’s rich precious metal region. (SRC_11)

- **November 20, 1873** — Several months earlier, on November 20, 1873, the party Alfred eventually joined set out from Bingham Canyon in Utah as part of a group of 20. (SRC_1)
  - This version places the expedition’s departure within a different account of the group Packer later joined. The discrepancy is important because the chronology contains competing descriptions of when the party formed and how large it was.
- **1873** — In the fall of 1873, Packer left with 20 prospectors on an expedition toward San Juan country. (SRC_4)
  - The expedition’s purpose was prospecting in the San Juan country, linking the men’s movements to the western mining rush. Their departure created the chain of events that eventually led to the disappearance and deaths of members of the party.
- **1873-1874** — Alfred Packer was accused of cannibalism during the winter of 1873-1874. (SRC_6)
  - The accusation went beyond ordinary murder allegations and involved the fate of the victims’ bodies. That charge made the episode exceptionally notorious and shaped how it was remembered.
- **1873** — Late in 1873, Packer joined a group of 20 prospectors headed to southwestern Colorado. (SRC_8)
  - This supplies another account of the expedition’s composition and destination. Alongside the other versions, it shows that the party’s size and exact departure circumstances were not recorded consistently.
- **1873** — After the war, Packer arrived in Utah in 1873. (SRC_8)
  - His arrival in Utah marks the transition from his postwar life into the western setting of the later expedition. Utah became the starting point associated with his movement toward southwestern Colorado.
- **August 1874** — In August 1874, a prospecting party discovered the bodies of the five missing men. (SRC_10)
  - The discovery ended the period in which the five men were merely missing. It provided the physical basis for the accusations and subsequent legal proceedings surrounding Packer.
- **1874** — Packer appealed for a retrial on the grounds that the crime was committed on the Ute Reservation in early 1874 and should therefore have been subject to federal rather than local jurisdiction. (SRC_10)
  - The appeal challenged not simply the verdict but the authority of the court that handled the case. Packer’s argument focused on the location of the alleged crime and the special status of the Ute Reservation.
- **January 21, 1874** — The party reached a Ute encampment near present-day Montrose on January 21, 1874, with its food and supplies exhausted. (SRC_10)
  - By the time the party reached the Ute encampment, its immediate ability to continue had been undermined by the loss of food and supplies. This marks a critical point of crisis before the later deaths.
- **January 1874** — Packer and five others ventured into the wilderness of the San Juan Mountains in January 1874 against the advice of Chief Ouray. (SRC_10)
  - Chief Ouray’s warning marked the danger of attempting the crossing in winter. The party’s decision to proceed placed them in the conditions that shaped the tragedy.
- **February 9, 1874** — Those five men followed Packer into a storm on February 9, 1874. (SRC_11)
  - Their departure left the men committed to a hazardous route through the mountains. Once the storm closed in, the group’s survival depended on finding food and shelter in an isolated wilderness.
- **1874** — Packer committed cannibalism during the winter of 1874 after guiding a group of five men into Colorado Territory’s San Juan Mountains during a snowstorm. (SRC_11)
  - The act became the central fact of Packer’s later notoriety. It also turned a failed prospecting journey into a story of survival, violence, and disputed necessity.

## 6. The Files

### The investigation `established`

The investigation began when a council was formed to investigate Alfred Packer’s story. As the meeting was beginning, two members of the Ute tribe arrived with pieces, or strips, of dried flesh that they said came from a human found on a hill near the agency. Alfred panicked, fainted, woke up, begged for mercy, and told a different story. He reportedly said, “It would not be the first time that people had been obliged to eat each other when they were hungry.” Alfred said that ten days into the journey the group ran out of food and the oldest man died first. According to Alfred, the remaining men ate the dead man and took his rifle. Five days later, another man died, the others ate him too, and they found $133 in his pockets. Alfred said another man died after that while he was gathering wood for the fire, and the remaining men ate him too. Alfred claimed Shannon Bell then shot the remaining men. He said that he and Shannon carried some of the dead man for food, hid the rest, and continued toward the agency. Alfred claimed Shannon later tried to kill him too, went mad, attacked him, and was shot by Alfred in self-defense. General Adams wrote Alfred’s confession down and had him sign it as a sworn statement; agency clerk Herman Lauter transcribed Alfred’s first official statement. Preston did not believe the confession and was sure Alfred had killed the men and stolen their things. Preston became so angry that he dragged Alfred outside, and others had to intervene to stop him from killing Alfred.

The historical accounts describe several reasons for Preston Nutter’s suspicion. Preston arrived in Saguache with two other original party members and found Packer at Dolan’s Saloon. Packer played high-stakes poker there and bought a $70 horse. Nutter found Packer’s story odd because Packer looked well-fed and because abandoning a guide in snowbound mountains seemed unlikely. Nutter also thought it was strange that Israel Swan would give Packer his rifle and leave four men with only one rifle for hunting. Nutter said Packer was sulky, obstinate, quarrelsome, and a petty thief. The accounts also say that Packer had been a poor guide, often got lost, argued with clients, and had a reputation for stealing and lying. Nutter said Packer had been constantly trying to find out how much money the men had. Packer had carried a Winchester rifle belonging to one of the missing men and a skinning knife belonging to Frank Miller, and he explained the knife by saying Miller had stuck it in a tree and walked away without it. Nutter and Loutsenhizer later tried to discredit Packer’s claimed qualifications as a guide, emphasizing his character flaws, inconsistent stories, and contradictions. The accepted theory at the time was that Packer had joined the party by overstating his qualifications as a mountain guide familiar with the area to accompany the men to Breckenridge; at best, that theory held, he led the party to miserable deaths through gross incompetence. A more popular theory was that he had left Ouray’s camp with the five men with a premeditated plan to lead them into the wilderness, kill them, and rob them.

The men persuaded General Adams to send a mounted agency officer to Saguache to retrieve Packer for questioning while pretending to recruit him for a search party. Packer was preparing to leave when Nutter and other men approached him again, and the agency officer arrived in time to separate the men. The officer told Packer he had been sent to enlist him as a guide for the search party. Packer agreed to return to Los Pinós because he had little alternative besides vigilante justice. At the agency, Packer met General Adams and five men he had not seen since February. Loutsenhizer demanded an explanation, and Packer repeated his earlier story. Packer acted amazed that the men had not been heard from and said he was concerned for their well-being. When asked where his money came from, Packer said he had received a cash loan from a man in Saguache. The officer also reported that Packer had arrived in Saguache with plenty of cash, several wallets, and possessions belonging to the missing men, while no one in town claimed to have lent him any money. Adams convened a council of himself, Packer, and the agency officers. The two Ute tribesmen then arrived with the strips of dried human flesh they had found on a hill near the agency while hunting. Packer begged for mercy and promised a full confession. General Adams called a conference of the five men and the agency officers to decide what to do next, and the five original party members said they did not believe a single part of Packer’s story.

The two Ute men said they knew of a place next to a lake that Packer had described, about 50 miles away across the hills. A search party led by Herman Lauter and consisting of the five Utah miners, several agency officers, and Packer as guide was assembled at once. After two weeks, near the Lake Fork of the Gunnison River, Packer said he was lost and did not recognize the surroundings. Nothing was found and the party returned to the agency. On the way back, Packer tried to murder Herman Lauter with a knife. Packer was caught, restrained, and arrested. General Adams, who had been willing to believe Packer until then, concluded that Packer was dangerous after the attack on Lauter. Packer was taken to Saguache and jailed outside town by the sheriff for his own protection. His jail was little more than a log cabin on ranch property belonging to the Saguache County Sheriff.

While Packer was in jail, observers challenged his credibility. The condition of the bodies contradicted Packer’s version of events. The bodies were all together in one spot rather than scattered across miles. Humphrey and Noon still had large portions of flesh, muscle, and organs that could have been eaten long before Bell allegedly tried to murder Packer. The men had tattered cloths lashed to their rotting feet, replacing shoes they had probably eaten. Moldy and tattered blankets lay beneath and beside the bodies. A beaten path led from the corpses to a crude shelter used by Packer. There was evidence that the deaths had occurred before supplies were totally exhausted, and inside the shelter were possessions of the men that Packer had left behind. The theory at the time was that Packer killed the men before supplies ran out to rob them, got snowed in, lived for months in his shelter, and walked to the bodies to cut off meat as needed.

In summer, General Adams led a search in the mountains to find the bodies, but the search failed. In August, illustrator John Randolph was crossing the mountains near Lake City when he noticed something sticking out of the ground near a tributary of the Gunnison River. John smelled something terrible and found five decomposed bodies that had met violent ends. One man was little more than bones, suggesting animals had gotten to him afterward. Two of the men had blunt force injuries to the head. Shannon Bell had been stripped to the bones except for his hands and feet and also had a blunt force head injury. The remains suggested deliberate removal of edible portions, and except for the first man, it did not look like animals had done it. John made a sketch and told authorities in Lake City. The local coroner and law enforcement, along with a team of 20 volunteers, later retrieved the bodies. The coroner concluded that the men had been killed by blows to the head while asleep. None of the bodies had defensive wounds or injuries, and all had similar markings on their skulls. The bodies had rags under their feet, suggesting they may have eaten the leather from their shoes after running out of food. There was also a track to a makeshift shelter, leading investigators to think Alfred may have killed the men for their possessions and eaten them to survive.

Other accounts identify Randolph as John A. Randolph, an illustrator for Harper’s Weekly, and place the discovery at the foot of Slumgullion Pass, two miles southeast of Lake City, in a pine-shaded gulch above the Lake Fork of the Gunnison River, a location later known as Deadman’s Gulch. One account says that the snow covering the bodies and campsite had melted during the intervening four months, and that the local coroner and law enforcement went to the site with about 20 volunteers. The bodies were in various states of decomposition and had been left to the elements and animals. The illustrator’s sketch was later published with the October 17, 1874, edition of Harper’s Weekly under the title “A Colorado Tragedy.” Most historians conclude that an illustrator for Harper’s Weekly found the remains near the Gunnison River while on assignment in the area. An illustrator who accompanied the group recorded the scene in visual graphic detail, helping draw national attention to the crime. The corpses showed intense violence and skull fractures consistent with blows from a hatchet. After officials examined the bodies, they buried the remains at the site, and the search party returned to Packer’s makeshift jail to confront him, only to find him missing.

Packer’s charges were expected to be upgraded from manslaughter to first-degree murder. Before that happened, someone gave Alfred a key, a penknife, and some food so he could escape. Packer allegedly received a makeshift key for his irons and some supplies, and escaped easily. Alfred slipped out of his cell and was not seen again for nine years. Months had passed with no definitive evidence of a crime, no bodies discovered, and no formal charges against Packer other than the attempt on Lauter’s life. Saguache County authorities were reportedly unhappy about taxpayer money being spent to keep Packer under guard. Nearly all of Saguache believed through rumor or deduction that Packer was guilty of robbery or murder, and Packer’s life was constantly threatened by nearby townspeople. Packer never said who helped him escape, how it happened, or why. It was presumed that his guard had been bribed by Packer or by someone else.

In March 1883, Jean “Frenchy” Cabazon encountered Packer when Packer approached him to buy supplies. Packer was living under the alias “John Schwartze” or “John Swartze.” Cabazon reported Packer to the local sheriff, who apprehended him and contacted General Adams. Cabazon was summoned to Cheyenne, confirmed Packer’s identity, and accompanied him by train to Denver for Packer’s second confession, which Packer signed on March 16. Packer said he fled because he feared mob justice from the people of Saguache. Because the actual crime was committed in Hinsdale County rather than Saguache County, Packer was sent to Lake City for detention and prosecution. Other accounts say Packer was jailed in Saguache, escaped, changed his identity, and was arrested in Wyoming before being returned to Hinsdale County for trial. After this arrest, Packer was tried and convicted of murder and sentenced to death, but the conviction was overturned on a technicality because Colorado became a state in 1876 and, before statehood, Colorado Territory had no statute under which he could be tried for murder. He was retried for manslaughter instead of murder and was eventually convicted of voluntary manslaughter. He received 40 years in prison, the maximum of eight years per man, which was described at the time as the longest prison sentence handed down in United States history. Other accounts say he was convicted of manslaughter in a second trial in Gunnison in 1886. He maintained that he had eaten the flesh of his companions to survive, but held to his story that Shannon Bell had killed the other men and that Packer killed Bell in self-defense.

In the century after the story, several groups conducted forensic analyses of the remains and compared them with Alfred’s account. Those groups tended to conclude that Alfred was guilty. Some of the forensic work found defensive wounds on bones from the group. One group found marks across Shannon Bell’s forearms, suggesting that he may have defended himself from an attack. In Alfred’s account, Shannon was only shot once and then finished quickly. The revolver was still loaded with three .38-caliber bullets, and the bullets matched some fragments of lead found in Shannon Bell’s hip. That evidence suggested that at least part of Alfred’s story may have been true.

The later forensic record is not consistent. On July 17, 1989, grad students dug up the skeletons in Lake City, Colorado, under hot, muggy conditions. When the first skull was exposed, it had a gaping hole in its forehead. Reporters rushed to Lake City’s four public pay phones to file a story headlined “Professor Exhumes 100-Year-Old Victims of Colorado Cannibal.” The team led by James Starrs, a professor of law and forensic science at George Washington University, unearthed the skeletons of five men from a mass grave in the San Juan mountains. Starrs led the exhumation project, which cost an estimated $10,000 to $15,000 and used modern forensic techniques including ground-penetrating radar and laboratory inspection of the remains. Starrs said the team’s examination showed that none of the victims had been shot, that four had been killed by hatchet blows to the head, and that the fifth skull had not been found. He said all five sets of bones revealed numerous knife marks indicating defleshing. He cited “unmistakeable proof of cannibalism,” said Packer had used the “natural refrigerator” of the Colorado winter to preserve his victims and eat them until spring, and declared, “He was having human flesh filets morning, noon and night.” More than a century after Packer was first accused of killing and eating five fellow prospectors in Colorado, Starrs said he had proven that Packer was “guilty as sin.” Starrs said Packer had “butchered his five fellow prospectors to death and then skinned them” and feasted on their flesh, and that the added scientific evidence convicted Packer “beyond any shadow of a doubt” in his opinion. Starrs said his aim was to settle the debate over Packer’s guilt and to “put forensic science on parade.” He believed Packer’s motive was initially to steal his companions’ belongings and then to turn to cannibalism.

The exhumation account says the remains were found at the end of a residential driveway at a home belonging to a local surgeon and were buried only 13 inches below ground. Starrs and Walter H. Birkby said they did not think cannibalism could ever be scientifically demonstrated because it would require a picture of the person actually eating human flesh. Packer never denied cannibalism, so the investigation did not focus on that point. The evidence was sufficient for Starrs to conclude that Packer had murdered his comrades. Starrs believed Packer more likely murdered his companions for their belongings and resorted to cannibalism out of necessity rather than intent. The remains were re-interred and given a proper burial with funeral rites.

Exhumation showed blunt force trauma to the skulls of two men, with no other notable disturbance except damage from butchering. All skulls had damage, to varying extents, in their upper craniums, and fabric fibers were found in some skulls, suggesting their heads may have been covered with a blanket at death. The three other skeletons showed defensive hacking marks across the radial and ulna bones of their forearms, including Shannon Bell’s, consistent with shielding the face and body from an attack. Shannon Bell was one of the skeletons with defensive forearm wounds, which could have been sustained while shielding his face and body from an attack. The skeletons showed no other noteworthy disturbance beyond damage made during butchering. All five skeletons had numerous differing post-mortem injuries, including depressed fractures, butterfly fractures, butchering trauma, and hacking trauma. Those post-mortem injuries appear to contradict Packer’s claim of minimal cannibalism.

Two skeletons other than Shannon Bell’s were found to have cylindrical puncture wounds in the pelvic bone. Some people have questioned whether all three pelvic marks were scavenging marks from a bear or possibly bullet wounds. No definitive conclusion has been made about the pelvic puncture wounds. One scenario suggested that two of the men were bludgeoned in their sleep by Packer. In the same scenario, the three remaining men awoke and Packer shot them in the hips to incapacitate them if the punctures were bullet wounds. The remaining men then may have tried to fight off Packer while he wielded a hatchet, and he killed them with blows to the head. Packer then butchered the men and used their flesh to survive in his snowbound state and during his journey. The different states of decomposition contradict that theory, although it is described as plausible. It seems unlikely that Packer shot three of the five men in the hip unless he was trying to debilitate them before killing them. Further analysis of entry and exit geometry could clarify the initial cause, but no such examination has been done to date. Packer’s ultimate motivation for the murders has been heavily debated and has several possible theories.

A different line of investigation concerns the pistol. In the Audrey Thrailkill collection of firearms at the museum was a Colt revolver reportedly found at the site of Packer’s alleged crime. An accession card said, “This gun found at the site where Alferd Packer killed and ate his companions.” Exhaustive investigation into the pistol’s background uncovered trial-era documents. A Civil War veteran who visited the crime scene said Shannon Bell had been shot twice and the other victims had been killed with a hatchet. The veteran said that, after studying Bell, he noticed a severe bullet wound to the pelvic area and a bullet hole through Bell’s wallet. This evidence seemed to corroborate Packer’s claim that Bell had killed the other victims and that Packer had shot Bell in self-defense.

In 1994, David P. Bailey, curator of history at the Museum of Western Colorado, began or reported an investigation into the pistol and the case; Bailey spent 10 years investigating the gun and the case. The pistol was described in one account as an 1862 Colt revolver, still loaded with three .38-caliber bullets, and in another as a rusted 1862 Colt Police Model .32 five-shot revolver found on Cannibal Plateau in the 1950s, with two shots missing. Dujay found microscopic lead fragments in soil taken from under Shannon Bell’s remains. Spectrograph analysis matched those fragments to the bullets remaining in Packer’s pistol. Analysis at Mesa State College’s Electron Microscopy Facility proved that bullet fragments exhumed from the burial site matched lead from the old pistol. Dujay’s team examined Bell’s samples and found a tiny lead fragment whose chemical composition matched the bullets in Bailey’s Colt revolver. Using a similar 1860s pistol, the team fired a bullet into an elk hip bone, and the resulting hole matched the hole in Bell’s hip bone. The analysis suggested that Packer did shoot Shannon Bell in self-defense, and Bailey believed the evidence supported Packer’s claim that he shot Bell. It now appears certain that at least Bell was shot, but whether Bell was shot in self-defense remains unanswered. Grant Houston remained skeptical because tourists had often found old guns and bullets near the massacre site and claimed they belonged to Packer or the other prospectors. Michelle Pierce was also unconvinced that Bailey’s pistol was the smoking gun.

Museum curator David Bailey said Packer did not deny eating the bodies, killed only in self-defense, and was wrongly convicted. That view conflicts with Starrs’s conclusion that Packer was guilty as sin and with the forensic interpretation that he murdered his companions for their belongings before resorting to cannibalism. Walter Birkby, the forensic anthropologist who disputed Starrs, said there was nothing to corroborate or refute anything Packer said and that no solid physical evidence would ever prove who did it. Michelle Pierce, Lake City’s former town clerk, believes Packer did not murder the five men. Pierce’s archival research turned up details that do not match the official narrative and portrays Packer as a pitiable soul whose social problems came from a misunderstood disease and harsh contemporary judgment. Pierce said, “People really disliked him,” probably because they were afraid of him. The text says that no clear or definitive evidence has yet been found for Packer’s deeds.

The Denver Public Library’s Western History/Genealogy Department is presented as a place to research Packer’s life and crimes. Researchers can use its materials to prove or disprove parts of the Packer legend. The collection includes microfilm of Denver and Colorado newspapers from the era, including complete runs of the Denver Post and Rocky Mountain News, as well as copies of Packer’s confession. The Colorado State Archives holds several legal and historical records concerning Alfred Packer. The Alfred Packer Collection includes penitentiary records, mugshot photos, parole records, confessions, letters to the governor concerning Packer’s parole, a governor’s pardon, original case files from Gunnison and Hinsdale District Court, Gunnison District Court cases 238–241, Hinsdale District Court cases 238–241, and Supreme Court case numbers 1198 from 1883, 1753 from 1885, 1980 from 1886, 3239 from 1893, and 3905 from 1898. The collection can now be viewed online: users are instructed to click the button for the online exhibits, choose “Browse Archive” at the top left below the State Archives logo, and select the Alfred Packer option.

By mid-April, only Packer emerged alive from the mountains. Ripley’s Vice-President of Exhibits and Archives, Edward Meyer, claimed that the museum possessed Packer’s dissected skull, bought from an anonymous party for a reported $20,000. No statement was made about how the museum verified the skull’s authenticity, other than that the seller’s information about its origin was sufficient. If the skull belonged to Packer, his head was removed shortly before or after burial, dissected with the brain removed for study, preserved by an arsenic curing process, displayed by a traveling sideshow, sold to a private party, and later sold to Ripley’s; as of 2008, it was reported to be at Ripley’s Museum in San Antonio, Texas, after being moved from the New Orleans facility following Hurricane Katrina. During the 1989 exhumation, a hole about the size of a thimble was found in Shannon Bell’s hip bone. James Starrs attributed the hole to scavenging animal fangs, while David Bailey wondered whether it had been made by a bullet.

### Packer’s final account `established`

On March 11, a man calling himself John Schwartze asked a shopkeeper to stock up for a planned trip. The shopkeeper thought the man looked familiar, then realized he was Alfred Packer. He told the local sheriff that he had spotted a criminal, and Alfred was arrested and taken to Denver. General Adams traveled to confirm Alfred’s identity and, after doing so, took him away for trial. Alfred then gave a final version of events that he largely kept until his death.

In that final story, when the group left Chief Ouray’s camp, they had only enough food for one grown man for a week. After two or three days, the group was hit by a brutal snowstorm. They kept trekking until they reached the base of a mountain and then a deep valley, but the snow on the far side was so deep they could barely walk. The party moved to higher ground and had only about a pint of flour left by day four. For about the next ten days, they lived only on rosebuds and pine gum while following a ridge above deeper snow. Several of the men were in such rough shape that they cried out every night and prayed to God to escape alive. They eventually crossed the main ridge and came down to camp by a river. The party followed the stream for a day until they reached a frozen lake, possibly Lake San Cristobal. They tried to cross the lake to reach trees for shelter, and by then they were exhausted and tensions were rising.

The narrative says Shannon Bell was furious at Alfred for losing the route and at the others for acting pathetic. According to Alfred, Shannon one morning ordered him to scout higher on the mountain for a way across. Alfred took a pistol and searched, but found only steep slopes and more snow. He admitted to General Adams for the first time that he did not really know what he was doing and did not know that side of the mountains. When Alfred returned after scouting all day, he found Shannon sitting by the fire eating a piece of meat. Alfred saw a man lying near the fire and the other three men lying motionless around it. Shannon rushed at Alfred with a hatchet, and Alfred shot Shannon in the stomach before finishing him after he fell. That night, Alfred later described being surrounded by “the midnight horrors of starvation as well as those of utter isolation.” He said he felt his sanity slipping and began to understand what had happened to Shannon because it was beginning to happen to him. In another letter, Alfred wrote, “The greatest wonder is that I ever returned to a rational condition.”

The next morning, Alfred tried to follow his tracks from the day before but turned back because of snow and exhaustion. He built a small shelter from wood and branches, but it did not satisfy his hunger. Alfred then cooked and ate some of the meat Shannon had been eating, realizing that he had to do so to survive. He said he then spent another two months in the shelter surviving off the rest of his party. Sixty days later, he saw an avalanche that left yellow clay behind, which told him he was in Slumgullion Pass. The pass is known for its yellow color and was named because it reminded early settlers of Slumgullion stew.

In early spring, Alfred finally knew where to go and set off up the mountain again. Before leaving, he gathered the remaining meat, wrapped it for the journey, and checked the dead men’s pockets, where he found the money he later spent in Saguache. He had already eaten his shoes, so he wrapped his feet in cloth and had to stop when they froze, lighting fires to warm them. As he neared the agency, he ate the last pieces of meat and may have dropped some later found by the Ute. Alfred said he had previously taken General Adams’s search party only as far as the mountains opposite the camp because he wanted to stop them from going farther and did not want to return to the horror. He also said he escaped and fled trial because he feared a mob would get past his guard and kill him. He said he went to Arkansas under a fake name, worked on a ranch, later moved to Arizona, and finally traveled to Cheyenne, where he was found.

Other sources preserve conflicting versions of this account. The reference article says it appears likely that Packer killed another prospector in self-defense before gnawing on the corpses of those who had already died. Packer later said that after three or four days without provisions, the group cooked and ate their rawhide moccasins. He also said the trail was entirely drifted over and that they gathered and ate the buds of wild rose bushes. After being re-captured, Packer said Shannon Bell killed James Humphrey, a prospector who left Ouray’s camp, George Noon, and Frank Miller while they slept around the campfire. Packer said Bell attacked him with a hatchet when he returned after searching for food, and that he fired twice with a pistol and shot Bell in self-defense. He said that after killing Bell he tried every day to get away but could not, so he lived on the flesh of the dead men for most of the sixty days he was out. He said the snow later formed a crust and he started out up the creek. Packer later confessed to living off the flesh of his companions and gave more than one version of the circumstances.

At the Los Pinos Indian Agency, Packer came during breakfast and begged for food and shelter. He carried a rifle, a knife, a steel coffee pot, and a satchel. When given food, he vomited and said his digestion had changed because of prolonged near-starvation. He told the agency that he had been hired by five men to guide them to Breckenridge. Packer said he became snow-blind during the journey and was left behind as a burden. He claimed Israel Swan gave him a rifle before the others abandoned him, and said he survived alone on roots and rose buds and ate his moccasins. The agency men thought his condition was odd because he did not look as malnourished and threadbare as expected after more than two months in the wilderness: his face was reported as bloated and his physique was hardly skeletal. Packer said he would drink whisky for breakfast instead of food.

Packer said he was broke and sold his Winchester rifle to Major Downer for $10. He stayed at the agency for ten days and then went to Saguache to buy supplies for a trip back to Pennsylvania. In Saguache, he bought a horse for $70 and booked a room at Dolan’s Saloon. Larry Dolan later said Packer spent around $100 during his stay and offered to lend him $300. Packer also spent $78 in Otto Mears’ general store. It was claimed that he had several different wallets in his possession. When Nutter asked where the rest of the party was, Packer said he had gotten his feet wet and frozen. Packer said the party camped when a winter storm started, that he made a fire while the others went ahead to find food, and that Swan left him with his rifle. He said the others never returned and that he then made his way through the mountains eating mostly rosebuds and small animals. Packer claimed Miller had stuck the knife in a tree and walked off without it.

In another account, Packer said the men left Ouray’s camp with what they thought was enough food for a 14-day journey, but provisions ran out sooner than expected. After a few days of no wildlife and almost no food, he said, the men began to eye each other unsettlingly. Packer said he returned from gathering firewood to find four men around Israel Swan’s body after Swan had been struck in the head with a hatchet and killed instantly. The four men butchered Swan, and Packer said he accepted the situation and joined them. Several thousand dollars was found on Swan and divided among the men. They ate the best parts of Swan, packed some away, and continued onward, with Packer taking Swan’s rifle.

Packer said the five men again ran out of meat within two days and could not find game. Bell, Humphrey, Noon, and Packer secretly decided that Miller would be next because he was stocky and had soft flesh. Miller was killed with a hatchet blow to the head while stooping to pick up firewood, then butchered and eaten. Packer said Humphrey was next to be sacrificed, followed soon after by George Noon. Only Bell and Packer were left, and they swore not to eat each other. They agreed to claim that the four other men had died from the elements and were buried with dignity, and vowed never to speak of the cannibalism because they believed no one would accept that it had been necessary.

Packer said that after a few days of travel with only a rabbit and some roots to eat, he and Bell camped beside a large lake next to a grove of hemlock trees. Bell later jumped from his blanket, screamed that he could not take it anymore, and said one of them would die for food. Packer said Bell rushed at him with a rifle to bash his skull in. Packer then butchered Bell, ate as much as he could, packed away more meat, took Bell’s share of Swan’s money, and continued alone. He said he threw away the remaining strips of Bell’s flesh because he hoped an animal would eat them.

Packer then said the men encountered a strong blizzard in the mountains. Heavy snow made them hopelessly lost and unable to retrace their route. They also ran out of matches and had to carry hot embers in a steel coffee pot to light fires. Days passed with no game and ice fishing failed completely. The men roasted and ate their shoes and tried to live on scant edible vegetation. Packer said the men made a pact that if one died, his meat would save the others from starvation. He said Israel Swan could go no further after days of hiking with almost no food; they found a pine-shaded gulch next to a lake and made camp there, and Swan then died of hunger and exposure.

Packer’s signed first confession said Swan died first and was eaten by the other five persons about ten days out of camp. It said Humphreys died four or five days later and was also eaten, that Humphreys had about $133, and that Packer found the pocketbook and took the money. The confession said the butcher was later killed, apparently accidentally, while Packer was carrying wood, and was also eaten. It said Bell shot California with Swan’s gun and Packer killed Bell. Packer said he shot Bell, covered up the remains, took a large piece along, and traveled 14 days to the agency. In that first confession, Packer said the first two deaths were circumstantial because of the rough conditions and that everyone in the party partook in cannibalism to stay alive. He said one man was “killed accidentally” while he was collecting firewood, and that Shannon Bell killed a man called “California,” after which Packer killed Bell in self-defense before traveling 14 days back to civilization.

In Packer’s later amended version, the men endured almost 20 days from Ouray’s camp and more than 10 days without substantial food. James Humphrey died of exposure to extreme cold, he said, and George Noon was killed days later by Shannon Bell solely for food. Bell and Packer agreed to stand together until the end and not eat each other. In this version, Bell later rushed at Packer with his rifle, intending to bludgeon him with the stock. Packer said he took the valuables of the deceased because they no longer needed them, but he did not specify the items or the amount of money obtained. He later said that only Bell and he remained after the other three men were dead.

In Packer’s second confession, he said Bell had killed the others after telling him to scout for a way out of the mountains and to find food. Packer said he returned in the late evening after being gone most of the day. Bell was roasting meat cut from Frank Miller’s leg; Miller’s skull had been crushed by a hatchet and lay furthest from the fire down the stream, while the other three bodies lay near the fire with hatchet cuts in the forehead. Packer said he dropped his revolver in deep snow after shooting Bell and lost it. He built a crude shelter from stray logs to fight the snow and wind and stayed away from the bodies. Another storm came, and he sheltered for hours while starving. He decided he had to eat something or die, returned to the fire, covered the men, took the piece of meat near the fire back to camp, cooked it, ate it, and lived off the flesh of the men for the bigger part of sixty days.

The same second-confession account says the party asked Packer to go up a nearby hill to see farther ahead for navigation. He returned to camp after a full day of going up and coming back down the hillside and said Bell had killed the entire camp and was eating the leg of a man roasted in the campfire. Bell rushed at him, and Packer shot Bell through the stomach with the rifle he had taken on the trip up the hillside. Packer said he made a new fire near his camp, cooked the piece of meat, and ate it. He tried to get away every day but could not, so he lived off the flesh of the men for the bigger part of the 60 days he was out. Packer’s third confession included more details but mostly the same information as the second confession.

The accounts also disagree over the weapon and the final struggle. One version says Bell had charged at Packer with Swan’s rifle and Packer shot him. Another says Packer finished Bell with a hatchet blow to the head, but that was the extent of his claim concerning the hatchet. In his second official version, Packer never claimed that he had fought off Bell with a hatchet. The account attributed to Packer says the party members died one at a time and were consumed by the other members until only he and Shannon Bell remained; Shannon, desperately hungry, tried to kill Packer before dying of natural causes, and Bell was killed in the ensuing fight. Packer said he had no choice but to eat Bell because Bell would have eaten him. He claimed Bell had murdered the others with hatchet blows to the head, maintained that he killed only Bell and did so in self-defense, and admitted to eating the flesh of his companions in order to survive. Under interrogation, Packer gave the first of multiple contradictory confessions, first claiming, “We ate old man Swann first.” He claimed the strong ate the weak one by one until only he and Bell remained, and that Bell tried to kill him, so he shot Bell dead. In one confession, he admitted to shooting one man in self-defense.

Packer’s written version said severe weather forced the men to camp in a ravine with some shelter from snow and wind. He wrote that they ate goatskin moccasins and rosebuds until they were weakened, emaciated, and mentally distracted, and that he was the one sane man among the six. He said he left camp to look for a way out and returned to find Shannon Wilson Bell roasting meat over a fire. Packer admitted surviving on the men’s flesh until he escaped the mountains. He wrote that his comrades were dead, he was left alone among starvation and isolation, and his body and mind were overwhelmed. In letters, he insisted that “the greatest wonder is that I ever returned to a rational condition.”

The accounts say Packer later maintained that Shannon Bell had killed the other men. After arriving alone at the Los Pinos Indian Agency, he said his comrades had left him while they searched for food. He claimed he did not know what happened to his companions even though he carried large sums of their money and possessed a rifle known to have belonged to one of them. Under interrogation, he admitted to partaking in cannibalism but denied committing murder. He complied with further questioning and repeated his story of self-defense and cannibalism for survival.

The wider account says that, because of his ineptitude, Packer led the men through the San Juan Mountains for nearly two months and covered only 250 miles at an average of three miles a day. In his correspondence, Packer tried to shift blame for the disaster onto Chief Ouray. He wrote that conversations with Ouray led them to believe they could reach the Los Pinos Agency in not over five days on foot across the mountains. Packer said he had never been to the agency and relied only on general reputation and information received from Ouray. The text calls Packer a known pathological liar and says his account to Newman about Ouray was not entirely truthful. Ouray never told Packer they were only five days from the Los Pinos Agency because at least three to four feet of snow blocked the path. Ouray did draw a crude map on the ground with a stick and gave them all the directions he could as a route. Packer wrote that they were given scanty fare—about 20 pounds of flour and not over 10 pounds of good meat—and that at the end of nine days the last provisions had been eaten. He said the group ate their goatskin moccasins and subsisted on rosebuds until they were weakened, emaciated, and mentally completely distracted. The text says those conditions led to the deaths and cannibalization of all in Packer’s party, while also calling Packer’s account ridiculous because he was living in a different territory under an alias to evade capture.

The later assessment remained disputed. Lamm said the evidence suggested that Packer killed only one man and only in self-defense, but added, “the proof was in the pudding and Alferd ate that, too.” Lamm told Tom Miller that, in the immortal words of Alferd Packer, “Well done!” After being accused of murdering the other men, Packer told a story that defined the rest of his life and earned him a permanent place in Colorado history.

### The trials `established`

At Alfred Packer’s first trial, witnesses testified that he had deliberately led the men astray with the intention of stealing their possessions by any means necessary. Preston Nutter, a prospector who rejected Packer’s account, testified that Packer had been skulking around, always trying to find out how much money each man had or was carrying. Other witnesses described Packer’s behavior after he reached Saguache and the different, conflicting stories he told about what had happened. The prosecution argued that his survival story was an attempt to cover up the killings. When Packer took the stand, he rambled for more than six hours and admitted eating the other members of the party, but said that he had killed only Shannon Bell and that Bell was responsible for everything else. The jury rejected his story and found him guilty of premeditated murder, theft, and several other charges. He was originally sentenced to death by hanging.

The dates and details of the first proceeding vary among the sources. One account says that the trial began on April 6 in Lake City; another dates its beginning to April 9, 1883, after seven days of testimony and examination. The prosecution presented about two dozen witnesses. Otto Mears testified that Packer possessed valuable Wells Fargo drafts when he emerged from the mountains. Constable Herman Lauter testified that Packer lunged at him with murder in his eyes when Lauter ordered him to hand over a knife. Packer called Lauter a liar and boasted, “To think that one little Dutchman could take a knife away from me. No, sir!” The jury reportedly reacted badly to Packer’s rambling testimony, his habit of speaking about himself in the third person, and his focus on personal grudges rather than on proving his innocence. One source says that the jury wasted little time finding him guilty of murder.

The first conviction was overturned because of a legal problem. Before statehood, Colorado Territory did not have a statute under which Packer could be tried for murder, and Colorado State established the specific laws only after the crime had taken place. The Colorado Supreme Court therefore held that the government could not sentence a man to death for a crime committed before Colorado became a state rather than a territory. The sources describe this as an ex post facto problem, a technicality, or the effect of an old territorial statute and a “savings clause.” Packer’s lawyer also assigned other errors to the court, including that Packer had not received a speedy trial and that the murder indictments came after what was then a three-year statute of limitations. Another account says Packer argued that the killings occurred on the Ute Reservation and should therefore have been subject to federal rather than local jurisdiction. The murder conviction and scheduled execution were overturned, so Packer had to be retried for manslaughter instead of murder.

A second trial was held in Gunnison after the Colorado Supreme Court granted a change of venue and a new Republican judge was appointed. Packer and his counsel argued that Lake City’s mainly Democratic community was too prejudiced for a fair trial. Of 57 prospective jurors interviewed, 54 admitted that they already knew about the case, and 44 said that they already believed Packer was guilty. Judge Melville Gerry ordered the sheriff to “scour the highways and byways” for jurors, and the sheriff filled the jury within a few hours. Packer was incarcerated at Gunnison while awaiting retrial, with one source saying that he waited nearly four years. He again pleaded not guilty and repeated his story.

The prosecution made the second case more damaging to Packer. Hunters testified that there had been plenty of food in the area even during a bad winter, including game, rabbits, deer, antelope, and more. They said that even someone as inexperienced as Packer could have hunted it easily. There was also a report that a deer carcass had been found near the campsite. That evidence weakened Packer’s claim that wildlife had been so scarce that the men quickly had to resort to cannibalism. The prosecution argued that the cannibalism therefore had to have been by choice because food would have been available. Witnesses again described Packer’s behavior after he reached Saguache and the different accounts he had given of the events. When Packer finally took the stand, he testified for more than six hours and admitted eating the other members of the party. His account of what happened at the campsite remained relatively close to his second official version. He asked to be charged the full 40 years only for Shannon Bell’s death, since Bell was the only killing he continued to claim, but the request was denied.

The prosecution argued that even if Packer was not a malicious monster who had premeditated the entire episode, he had still led the group astray by taking a more dangerous winter route while pretending to be a guide. The text says that, at best, he was extremely negligent. Packer was convicted of manslaughter and received 40 years in prison, described as the maximum of eight years per man. The sources disagree on the legal description: Packer’s criminal record lists “voluntary manslaughter” on five counts, while other accounts call the conviction involuntary manslaughter. They agree that the sentence was 40 years, or eight years for each of the five men. At the time, one source calls it the longest prison sentence ever handed down in U.S. history, while another calls it the longest custodial sentence in U.S. history. He was sentenced to 40 years of hard labor at the Colorado State Penitentiary in Cañon City.

The sources also give different summaries of the sequence. One says that Packer was arrested on murder charges, escaped from the Saguache jail, remained on the run for eight years, was captured in Wyoming in 1883, and was returned to Lake City, where he was tried, found guilty of murder, and sentenced to hang. Another says that he escaped and hid from justice for nine years, and still another says that he spent nearly a decade on the run before authorities tracked him down. A separate account says he escaped before trial and was eventually found guilty of Bell’s murder in 1883. The consistent outline is that local authorities did not believe his story, that he escaped from jail, that he was later captured in Wyoming and brought back to Hinsdale County, and that he was eventually tried and convicted of murder before winning a retrial on a technicality.

Judge M. B. Gerry is associated with the famous quotation supposedly spoken while sentencing Packer in the first trial. In the legendary version, Gerry said that Packer had come to Hinsdale County when there were seven Democrats, had eaten five of them, and was sentenced to hang. One version has Gerry saying, “I sentence you, Alfred Packer, to be hanged by the neck until you are dead, dead, dead.” Another gives the more elaborate wording, “Stand up yah voracious man-eatin’ sonofabitch and receive yir sintince. When yah came to Hinsdale County, there was siven Dimmycrats,” followed by the claim that Packer had eaten five of them and was being sentenced to hang as a warning against reducing the Democratic population of the state. Gerry reportedly said that there were seven Democrats in Hinsdale County and that Packer had eaten five of them, and he reportedly sentenced Packer to hang until he was “dead, dead, dead” as a warning against reducing the Democratic population of the state.

The Colorado history account says that the legend is apocryphal. Fred M. Mazzula argues in his book \*Al Packer, A Colorado Cannibal\* that Judge Gerry did not talk like Yosemite Sam. Mazzula used the trial transcripts to determine what Gerry actually said at sentencing. The actual sentence ordered that Packer be taken by the sheriff of Hinsdale County to a place of execution within the corporate limits of Lake City, between 10 a.m. and 3 p.m. on May 19, 1883, and that he be hanged by the neck until he was “dead, dead, dead.” It ended with a request that God have mercy on Packer’s soul. The text says Gerry’s real quotation is less colorful but more accurate. One source nevertheless records that Gerry sentenced Packer to be “hung from your neck until you are dead, dead, dead.”

Lake City residents were outraged when the death sentence was overturned and threatened to hang Packer themselves, so the second trial was moved to Gunnison, about 50 miles away. Packer escaped death on a technicality and received the new trial. After the new proceeding, he was convicted of manslaughter and sentenced to 40 years in the Colorado State Penitentiary in Cañon City. After a confession, a search for the bodies, and a trial, one article summarizes, Packer was sentenced to forty years at the Colorado State Penitentiary. The sources differ over whether the second-trial conviction should be called voluntary or involuntary manslaughter, but Packer’s listed convictions are murder, overturned, and voluntary manslaughter on five counts, and his listed criminal penalty is death, later commuted to 40 years’ imprisonment.

The prosecution’s motive theory also focused on Israel Swan. At the time of Packer’s trial, Swan’s family reported that he had left for the expedition with about $6,000 in cash and gold and a valuable Winchester rifle. The money and rifle would have provided a strong motive for murder, and Swan was 65 at the time of his death. Packer was not found with any gold when he was arrested. Although he had some money left, it did not total in the thousands, and the sources say that his spending had been frivolous. One account says that he spent more than $100 on drinking and gambling and another $78 in a general store, while often giving conflicting stories when he drank. The prosecution argued that the only logical reason for him to travel through the mountains with so little food and supplies was to lead the men into the wilderness to kill and rob them. Packer pleaded not guilty.

The first-trial evidence concerning the deaths included the prosecution’s claim that Swan died on or around March 1. In court, the prosecution argued that Swan showed signs of a struggle at death and that the other men appeared to have been killed in their sleep. Otto Mears, Larry Dolan, Oliver D. Loutsenhizer, and Preston Nutter testified for the prosecution, with Nutter as the key witness. Other accounts repeat that Nutter testified that Packer had been trying to learn how much money each man was carrying. The physical evidence was also used against Packer: the men had no defensive wounds or injuries in one account, while later exhumation accounts describe defensive hacking marks on the forearms of three skeletons, including Shannon Bell’s. All five skeletons showed varying post-mortem injuries, including depressed fractures, butterfly fractures, butchering trauma, and hacking trauma. These findings leave the evidentiary picture disputed rather than settled.

Packer’s lawyer mounted what one source calls a spirited defense, but Packer went to prison for seventeen years before \*The Denver Post\* petitioned for his release. In prison, he made horsehair bridles, and one of them is displayed at the Museum of Western Colorado in Grand Junction. He also built elaborate Victorian dollhouses. After being imprisoned, Packer wrote to anyone he could, claiming that his sentence was unjust, that he had killed only Shannon, and that the jury was full of biased and prejudiced people. He filed appeals five times and was denied each time. He sent letters to local newspapers saying that he had been unjustly convicted by an unfair and unsympathetic judicial system and by ignorant, small-minded people. On June 19, 1899, the Colorado Supreme Court officially upheld his sentence. He never succeeded in obtaining an official state pardon, although the Colorado State Archives collection includes a governor’s pardon and also contains penitentiary, mugshot, parole, confession, governor’s-parole, and original court records.

The sources disagree about the length of time Packer served and the date of his release. One says he served 15 years before being granted parole; another says he spent 16 years in prison; another says he served 17 years before \*The Denver Post\* petitioned for his release. The account based on the Colorado State Archives says that Alfred was paroled in 1901, 18 years into his sentence. Another source says that former Governor Charles Thomas paroled him in 1901, while a separate historical summary gives 1905. The sources agree that he was paroled rather than officially pardoned and that Polly Pry, a reporter for \*The Denver Post\*, helped campaign for his release. After his parole, he worked for \*The Denver Post\* as a guard and later as a ranch hand.

Packer’s criminal status is listed as deceased. His convictions are listed as murder, overturned, and voluntary manslaughter on five counts; his criminal penalty is listed as death, later commuted to 40 years’ imprisonment. The text says that Packer is one of four people historically convicted for cannibalism in the United States. It also describes the incident as the only known trial in the United States associated with human cannibalism, while stressing that Packer was not actually convicted of cannibalism as such: he was tried and found guilty of murder in the first trial and manslaughter in the second. The sources state that cannibalism itself was not necessarily illegal in the United States unless a person committed murder to obtain the flesh.

Packer’s supporters continued to dispute the verdict. Polly Pry portrayed him as a former soldier and a man of the people who had been persecuted by the establishment, had been in the wrong place at the wrong time, and had survived horrors that most people could not imagine. She argued that his sentence was so harsh only because of the cannibalism and that anyone would have done the same in his situation. Her stories changed the minds of some local businesses and residents, leading them to organize petitions for his release. In mock trials held in Lake City, Boulder, and other Colorado towns, actors played the main roles and audience members served as jurors. When David Bailey’s evidence was included, jurors acquitted Packer more often than not. The State Archives collection includes a governor’s pardon, but the historical accounts say that Packer never obtained an official state pardon.

### The expedition `established`

The sources give differing accounts of the expedition’s size and divisions. One historical account says Robert McGrue, leader of the larger prospecting party, organized a party of 20 to travel from Salt Lake City to the San Juan Mountains after hearing of gold discovered in Breckenridge. Another account says that a party of 21 prospectors, led by Alferd—or Alfred—Packer, left Bingham Canyon, Utah, on November 8, 1873, hoping to be among the first to locate mining claims in the San Juan region. A separate account describes Packer as part of a larger band of 20 gold seekers who left Utah and split into two groups. The prospectors were drawn by news of a gold strike and reports of enormous fortunes, and one newspaper account says they were promised “marvelous tales of enormous fortunes to be had for the mere asking.” Their intended route was toward Breckenridge, by way of the Los Piños Indian Agency.

Packer had worked as a guide for only a few months, and the accounts disagree about how much responsibility to assign him, but they agree that the party progressed more slowly than expected toward Breckenridge despite his serving as guide. One account says Alfred managed, by luck, to get the party only as far as just outside Montrose. The journey from Utah to Colorado was slow and difficult: winter arrived early and severely, the wagons and horses were crippled, and heavy snow along the Spanish Trail covered the path so completely that the men relied almost solely on a compass. The wagons could hardly follow a trail, and Packer’s inexperience showed itself as the party became lost. By January, provisions had run out completely. The men first survived on horse feed and came close to considering the horses themselves as food. The men later described Alfred as greedy, lazy, and constantly fighting with someone; other accounts add that he was argumentative, inexperienced, distrusted, and known for lying and theft.

On January 21, 1874, the party found a Ute encampment in a valley just outside Montrose, in the Uncompahgre Valley, led by Chief Ouray, who was known as “The White Man’s Friend.” The accounts say Ouray supplied the men with food, lodging, and shelter, exchanged food and supplies with them, and offered to let them remain with his tribe until winter had passed. He recommended postponing the expedition until spring because the winter weather in the mountains was dangerous, promised to share all that he and his people had with them, and warned that no Ute would attempt the journey in those conditions. According to the sources, he said that trying it meant certain or almost certain death. The party still had about 120 miles and the San Juan Mountains left to cross. One account records a different claim attributed to Packer: that Ouray said the Los Piños Agency could be reached in no more than five days on foot across the mountains. The same account says that this was not truthful, because three or four feet of snow blocked the route; men who remained at the camp said Ouray told the prospectors they would all die and that no Indian in his band would go to show them the route because the deep snow was too dangerous.

Despite the warning, 11 men decided to continue. They planned to travel first to the Los Piños Indian Agency, the closest outpost to Ouray’s camp, and then proceed to Breckenridge. Ouray provided food for the journey and safe directions to bypass the mountains, advising them to drop down to the Gunnison River and follow it north to the agency. The party divided: one account says five men followed Oliver Loutsenhizer along the Gunnison River while six others, including Packer, headed through the San Juan Mountains. Loutsenhizer threatened to shoot Packer if Packer tried to follow the other group. Another account describes three factions at the Ute encampment: some men paid Ouray a trespassing fee and stayed with the Utes for the rest of the winter; others accepted a week’s provisions from him and traveled toward the Los Pinos Agency in Saguache County; and a third group ignored his warnings. The river-bound group nearly perished after losing its way, while some of the men stayed behind with the horses and wagons until spring.

Packer insisted that crossing the mountains directly was better than going around them because he claimed to know the area and said the route was faster and more direct. The sources emphasize that locals had given him a safer route beside a main water source, where the men could have caught fresh fish and stayed on course. Instead, Packer chose the mountain route with minimal supplies because he believed it would be faster. Although the route he and his companions took was nearly identical in length to the recommended route, though not in its traverse, it was far more dangerous. The choice to hike through the San Juan Mountains in midwinter—where snow could exceed six feet in a single snowfall, with blasting winds and freezing temperatures—is described as practically suicidal.

After fierce snowstorms boxed in the expedition, many of the men returned home, but six, including Packer, continued. On February 9, Packer and five others left for the Los Piños Indian Agency, described in one account as 75 miles ahead. The five men were George Noon, Shannon Wilson Bell, James Humphrey, Frank Miller, and Israel Swan; they wanted to reach Breckenridge before thousands of fortune hunters arrived. Their immediate goal was the agency, followed by Breckenridge. The group traveled southeast and then south along the Lake Fork, passing the site that would become the townsite of Lake City within six months. After wandering, the party was discovered by warpainted Ute Indians, and Chief Ouray took them to a spot near his village where they established a makeshift camp. When Ouray realized that Packer’s party would still enter the storm, he supplied them with provisions despite the warnings.

Packer’s men entered the mountains with barely enough food for the supposed 14 days required by the safest route. They had no snowshoes, minimal matches, no flint, no heavy clothing, two rifles, one pistol, a couple of knives, a hatchet, and minimal ammunition. Another account says Packer had left Utah with few provisions and no weapons. The larger party’s river route initially followed Ouray’s directions under Bob McGrue’s guidance, but the horses eventually could not continue. McGrue unloaded the men’s provisions and returned to Ouray’s camp. Packer then took his party higher into the San Juan Mountains against Ouray’s warning.

The river party had a brutal journey in the cold. The freezing winter made the Gunnison route difficult, and the men ran out of food long before reaching the agency. Cowhands found them and fed them at a cattle camp until spring; another account says the cowhands let them remain there until spring. In April, when the weather improved, the men reached the agency in May and found that Alfred had already been there alone. The sources also describe Packer’s separate arrival: he wandered into the Los Pinos Agency in Southwest Colorado three months later, on April 16, 1874, 65 days after his departure, and another account places the agency 75 miles ahead. He emerged from the woods alone, carrying a pocket full of cash, a rifle, and a coffee pot, with no traveling party, which aroused suspicion. The account that gives his arrival as April 16 says he entered the agency during a snowstorm, begging for food and shelter.

A video dramatization presents Packer as the sole survivor of a six-man party that tried to cross the San Juan Mountains in the Colorado Territory; the underlying historical accounts likewise say he emerged as the sole survivor. Those accounts say the six men lost their way and became snowbound, that five prospectors died agonizing deaths in the mountain cold, and that a sixth man had his life irrevocably altered by the events. One account says that only Packer returned in the winter of 1874. The expedition account also records that Jean “Frenchy” Cabazon had been a member of the original party that left Provo, wisely stayed in Chief Ouray’s camp, and later reached the destination safely with Bob McGrue and Preston Nutter’s party.

The men neared the point of eating the horses themselves.

### Packer’s legacy `established`

Packer’s story of cannibalism is presented as a danger story about nineteenth-century prospectors in the Rocky Mountains and as one of the West’s most grisly and enduring legends and murder mysteries. The case reflects the eagerness of prospectors to trespass into the Ute reservation, illustrates how swiftly development took place once the San Juan region opened, and attracted national and international attention to Lake City and its environs. Local papers gave the case constant coverage, using sensational headlines, negative comments about Packer’s character, and imaginative theories that drew national and international attention. Harper’s Weekly coverage also drew national attention to the grisly crime; an illustrator from Harper’s Weekly recorded the scene in visual graphic detail, and copies of the portrait photograph were sold for 50 cents to the curious.

The area northeast of Lake City where Packer’s party got lost is labeled “Cannibal Plateau” on maps, and the site where the bodies were found five miles beyond town is called Deadman’s Gulch. The text says Packer evolved from Old West infamy to New West celebrity. His pistol is now on display at the Museum of Western Colorado. In Lake City, “Al Packer Days,” the Packer Burger at the Cannibal Grill, and a wooden marker for the Alferd Packer Massacre Site are popular attractions. Travel magazines say the Hinsdale County Museum has the largest collection of Packer memorabilia known, including skull fragments, clothing buttons from victims, and shackles used when Packer was imprisoned. Tourists are advised not to miss the actual burial site, which is five minutes from town.

The modern myth states that Packer was convicted of cannibalism, but the text says that belief is totally incorrect. He was tried and found guilty of murder in the first trial, and of manslaughter in the second. Nearly all of Saguache and soon nearly the entire country and beyond believed Packer’s responsibility for the deaths was beyond doubt. Cannibalism was not seen as the main issue of Packer’s guilt. People then knew the Donner Party story and understood to some degree the need to eat in the wilderness. Cannibalism was and is not illegal per se in the United States unless a murder is committed to obtain the flesh. If someone murders to obtain flesh, the charge is murder, with the cannibalism treated as desecration or abuse of a corpse. Packer claimed for the rest of his life that he was unjustly vilified and convicted for cannibalism rather than for murder, which he continued to deny.

The sources disagree about parts of Packer’s punishment and later life, and those disagreements remain part of the legacy. One account says Packer had served 18 years of his 40-year sentence, while another says he served 16 years of that 40-year sentence before being released. The latter account says he was originally sentenced to death, received a 40-year sentence after a second trial in 1885, and lived out the rest of his life in relative peace. Another source says he was convicted of five counts of voluntary manslaughter in Gunnison in 1886 and sentenced to 40 years. The text says Packer ultimately found some redemption from the people of Colorado, or at least from the justice system. It also says that he was the most celebrated Colorado convict, wooed and loved despite his life sentence for eating his camping compadres.

Packer’s name and story entered books, film, music, restaurants, and jokes. In Dashiell Hammett’s novel The Thin Man, Nick Charles advises Gilbert Wynant to read a selection about Packer’s expeditions. That selection comes from Thomas Samuel Duke’s Celebrated Criminal Cases of America, where Thomas Samuel Duke gave Packer’s first name as Alfred. Harold Schechter wrote the true-crime book Man-Eater about Packer. In 1993, CU film students Matt Stone, a CU film student, and Trey Parker, a CU film student, produced Cannibal! The Musical; Matt Stone and Trey Parker later created the television show South Park. Cannibal! The Musical was Trey Parker’s directorial debut, and the text says it was short-lived, like Packer’s companions. The story is being dramatized in the film rather than simply reported, and the underlying account is Packer’s expedition and its aftermath.

The University of Colorado Boulder has an Alferd Packer Grill where students are greeted by a giant portrait of Packer and the slogan “Have a friend for lunch!” The cafeteria grill’s slogan was “Have a friend for lunch!”, customers can order an El Canibal beefburger, and a giant map outlining Packer’s travels through Colorado was displayed on the wall. The grill has since been renamed the Alferd Packer Restaurant & Grill. A commenter says they first learned about Packer while attending CU Boulder and learning how the Alferd E. Packer Grill got its name. UC-Boulder students have hosted Al Packer Days wearing T-shirts with slogans like “Finger lickin’ good” and “When you care enough to eat the very best.” The text mentions Alferd Packer’s High Protein Gourmet Cookbook as an example of gallows humor about the story, but also says there is really nothing funny about the tale.

The University of Colorado example was echoed in Washington. At a press conference on August 10, 1977, Secretary of Agriculture Bob Bergland and his employees unveiled a plaque naming the executive cafeteria “The Alferd Packer Memorial Grill.” Bergland said Packer’s life exemplified the spirit and fare of the cafeteria and would “serve all mankind.” Barbara Walters covered the event on ABC Evening News. The stratagem succeeded and the contracts were terminated soon afterward. Bergland removed the plaque after accepting the bureaucratic objection that it lacked official GSA authorization. The plaque is now displayed on the wall of the National Press Club’s The Reliable Source members-only bar, and the National Press Club’s hamburger is called the Alfred Packer Burger.

Phil Ochs, a 1960s folk-music songwriter, used humor in “The Ballad of Alferd Packer” (1964), matching the lighthearted ongoing attitude toward Packer and his alleged crimes. The song never appeared on any of Ochs’s studio or live albums. C. W. McCall wrote the song “Comin’ Back for More” about Packer. Packer remains a wildly popular figure in Colorado state history. A restaurant in Boulder, Colorado, called the Alferd Packer Grill was named in his honor, and events and traditions honoring Packer take place annually in Colorado and 12 other states.

Lake City today treats Packer as both its patron saint and its antihero. Lake City is a remote mountain town at an elevation of 8,661 feet. Most visitors come to hunt, fish, or hike, and some hope to glimpse Al Packer’s ghost. The town has businesses called the Packer Saloon & Cannibal Grill and the Cannibal Cabins. Lake City has hosted an annual Al Packer Day with coffin races, mystery-meat eating competitions, and an Al Packer look-alike contest. Grant Houston of the Hinsdale County Historical Society says many local people, including himself, would say Packer was guilty. Houston says local tradition held that Packer lured the men up there for money or guns he coveted, killed them, and fled. The courthouse contains Packer memorabilia, newspaper accounts, and official trial records.

Packer’s grave in Littleton has been left with offerings such as a toy firetruck, a marijuana vape pen, and a vase for flowers. A wooden bench invites visitors to sit and reflect at Packer’s grave. Packer was buried in Littleton, Colorado. The sources differ on the exact circumstances and date of his death: one says he died in 1907 at a ranch near Deer Creek in western Jefferson County, another says he died two years after becoming a local celebrity in Gunnison, and another says he died alone in Littleton at age 69. His death was cited as “Dementia – trouble & worry,” though his clinical cause of death has also been described as a stroke. Another source says his official cause of death was listed as “Senility -- trouble and worry.” Packer was widely rumored to have become a vegetarian before death and was reported by acquaintances as a man rich with stories and liked by children. He lived modestly and was reported to be charitable. There was also a rumor that Alfred was a vegetarian when he died.

After prison, Alfred worked at the Denver Post as a guard for a while. People described Alfred after prison as kind, modest, and charitable, in contrast with the grumpy, greedy, and untrustworthy man he had been in the past. Alfred eventually moved to Littleton, where he bought a copper mine and returned to prospecting. Another account says Packer lived in Denver, then moved to Littleton to be closer to the mountains, bought copper claims, and resumed prospecting until epilepsy overcame him. He remained a free man for nine years after the escape; while in Gunnison, he became a local celebrity; and Packer lived the rest of his life in relative peace.

The story of Packer and the story that actually occurred are not always the same. A commenter objects to what he calls revisionist history and says he wants to keep the lore of the Old West without modern filtering. The text says Packer’s legacy remained unsettled even after his peaceful death. It also says that the tale has been the subject of gallows humor and amusements, but that five men died agonizing deaths in the cruel mountain cold while a sixth had his own life irrevocably altered, so there is really nothing funny about the tale.

The sources also discuss Packer’s female admirers through the idea of hybristophilia. Mental health professionals call the disorder in which some women become infatuated with heinous serial killers and mass murderers in jail cells hybristophilia. Popular culture refers to hybristophilia as Bonnie and Clyde syndrome. The condition is usually characterized by a woman communicating with a high-profile criminal in an attempt to kindle a relationship, and the text says hybristophilia is not solely a 20th-century phenomenon. Alfred Packer, also spelled Alferd, received fan mail from several adoring women while he was incarcerated for his crimes. Before Pry helped secure his release, Packer received a constant stream of curious visitors who came to ogle Colorado’s nationally known prisoner. He also received many letters from adoring well-wishers while in prison.

One sympathizer was Mrs. Rebecca A. Newman. Newman worked as a schoolteacher while living in the Bronx neighborhood of New York City. By opening communications with Packer, Newman apparently hoped to form a friendship with him and express sympathy for his circumstances. When Governor-Elect Alva Adams learned of Newman’s affection for Packer, he warned her that if she knew Packer and his horrible crime she would not care to defend him or excuse his conduct. Not dissuaded by Adams, Newman insisted that communications be opened anyway. Newman asked Packer for a full detailed statement of the whole circumstances connected with his most extraordinary trials and sufferings.

While in prison, Packer often crafted leather belts, bridles, wooden canes, and watch fobs from dyed and braided horsehair. Packer usually sold those items as souvenirs to prison visitors and sometimes gifted such items to people he thought were special. Packer gave Rebecca a chain bracelet as a sign of affection and desire for a relationship. Through his correspondence with Newman, Packer hoped to find out what she could do for him. Packer wrote that he would make her proof he was worthy of the aid of philanthropic people and those better situated than he was financially and otherwise. The text says women like Newman still exist today, that contacting famous criminals who are experienced manipulators is a dangerous game, and that people who do not proceed with caution sometimes end up being eaten alive.

### Polly Pry’s campaign `established`

Alfred Packer’s story eventually reached Leonel Campbell Ross O’Bryan, who used the pen name Polly Pry. She was a controversial reporter for The Denver Post and later a freelancer in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and was known for her connection to the Alfred Packer case. Pry became the Denver Post’s first female reporter in 1898 and was a member of the Denver Woman’s Press Club. At age fifteen, she eloped to Mexico with the railroad industrialist George Anthony; five years later, she left Anthony and moved to New York to become a reporter. She was initially rejected by The New York Times, but later obtained a trial assignment there. Fellow reporters gave her the nickname Polly Pry because they said she could pry information out of anyone.

One account says Pry first met Packer and interviewed him while researching an article about Colorado’s prison system and the Colorado State Penitentiary; a biographical account dates that meeting to 1901. After hearing Packer’s story, she partly believed him and used her position as a reporter to try to help him. She believed he was innocent of murder and advocated for him in print throughout the trial. In the early 1900s, she launched a journalistic and media campaign to publicize his plight and release him. She wrote stories presenting him as a man of the people: an ex-soldier persecuted by the establishment, a common man caught in a regrettable situation, someone in the wrong place at the wrong time who had survived horrors most people could not imagine. She argued that Packer’s sentence was so harsh because of the cannibalism, and claimed that anyone would have done the same in that situation. In her column, she also argued that since sailors were legally allowed to eat people when lost at sea, the same rules should apply to people stranded in the mountains. Another account says she used Packer’s Army service to portray him as a common man who had been caught in a regrettable situation, had done what he had to do to survive, and had been crucified for violating civilized sensibilities by resorting to cannibalism.

Over time, Pry’s stories led local businesses to arrange petitions for Packer’s release. They also led to petitions and requests reaching Colorado Governor Charles Thomas’s office, although those efforts met strong opposition. Packer gained allies who signed a petition for his pardon. One supporter wrote to Thomas that, even if Packer had committed the crime, his privations made responsibility difficult to assign: “The line between sanity and insanity is often very thin, and I don’t think we understand it.” The Colorado State Archives says its Alfred Packer Collection includes letters to the Governor concerning Packer’s parole, as well as parole records and a Governor’s pardon. Packer had made many appeals for a pardon after entering prison but was repeatedly denied. A separate account says he filed five appeals and was roundly denied each time. In 1899, when Denver Post writer Polly Pry visited him at the Colorado State Penitentiary in Canon City, she wrote that “the corroding desire for freedom has eaten into his heart” and that “the blind injustice of the world has seared into his soul.”

The sources give differing accounts of the outcome and date. One says that Governor Thomas ultimately relented and, in his last official act before leaving office, paroled but did not pardon Packer on the condition that Packer would not try to profit from his story. Another says that, as his last act in office, Thomas granted parole but not pardon. A historical account says Packer received parole on February 8, 1901, after a campaign initially spearheaded by his old acquaintance Duane Hatch, who petitioned for nearly a decade before his efforts came to Pry’s attention. A different account says Packer received parole in 1905, while another says Pry’s campaign and Harry Tammen’s intervention eventually led to a pardon on January 7, 1901. The sources therefore do not agree on whether he was pardoned or only paroled, or on the date. One account says Packer had served 18 years of a 40-year sentence; another says he was granted parole after serving 15 years.

Denver Post publishers Fred Bonfils and Harry Tammen hired a lawyer to defend Packer. Because Pry’s Packer column increased the paper’s revenue and readership, one account says she persuaded Tammen to pay for Packer’s defense and demand a retrial. After parole, Packer worked as a guard at The Denver Post and later as a ranch hand. Many people believed that his Denver Post job came directly from Pry and her employers securing his release. Packer showed enduring respect for Pry and called her his “Liberator.”

After the trial, Pry was present during a confrontation involving W. W. Anderson, Packer’s attorney, and the owners of The Denver Post who had hired him. The owners learned that Anderson had taken a double payment and demanded that he return the money. During the confrontation, Anderson pulled a gun and shot both men. As he prepared to shoot again, Pry jumped in front of Harry Heye Tammen and tried to shield him. She stalled Anderson long enough for the police to arrive and used her skirt to stop the bleeding. Rumors later circulated that Pry’s skirts had saved her publishers’ lives by slowing bullets from the gun. When union workers boycotted The Denver Post over her stance on labor issues and immigration, she founded her own paper, Polly Pry. Afterward, gunmen attacked her at home rather than at work. Pry was a strong advocate of free speech and, during World War I, came to the aid of French war orphans.

Pry’s involvement has also been dramatized. Two film versions of the Alferd Packer story included her. In Jim Roberson’s The Legend of Alfred Packer (1980), Cynthia Nessin portrayed Polly Pry. The film included the Anderson incident, but the man was not named and was called “Gunman” in the credits. After Pry begins telling Packer’s story, she does not appear again in the film, and the shooting is not mentioned again. The film depicts her as hard-drinking and forceful. Trey Parker’s Cannibal! The Musical featured Toddy Walters as Polly Pry and went more deeply into her involvement with the case, but ignored the fact that she was married in order to make her a love interest for Packer.

### The missing men `established`

According to the expedition leader, Alfred told him that after leaving the Ute camp, his party followed the river on horseback until the horses could go no farther. The leader unloaded their provisions and walked the horses back to the Ute camp. The men then likely walked farther down the river and took a path high into the mountains. The leader knew they did not have enough supplies to reach the agency, but he had grown tired of arguing with Alfred. They had only about 14 days of food, inadequate clothes, no snowshoes or flints, and hardly any matches. Their equipment consisted of only a few rifles, one pistol, a hatchet, and some knives. After the leader turned back, no one heard from those men again until Alfred appeared at the agency months later.

When Alfred arrived, he looked bloated and well-fed, unlike the starving men people usually saw coming out of the wilderness. After he felt better, he went to Saguache, where he rented a room and a saloon and spent over $100 on drinking and gambling. He spent another $78 in the general store. People said he gave conflicting stories when he drank, sometimes carried a different wallet, and never seemed to run out of cash. He had sold his rifle at the agency for only $10, which did not explain his remaining money. Preston Nutter, the leader of the other party that followed the river, arrived at the agency in May and later went to Saguache for answers. Preston suspected Alfred was lying and told General Adams that the other men would not have left him for dead. The rifle Alfred sold belonged to an old man in the group, and the pipe Alfred left behind belonged to Shannon Bell. When Alfred originally joined the group, he had no money.

General Adams sent a rider to bring Alfred in for questioning. When questioned, Alfred said he got his feet wet and cold, stayed by the fire while the others looked for food, and assumed they had abandoned him when they did not return. A separate account says that nine weeks later Packer arrived at the Los Piños Indian Agency south of present-day Gunnison with a Winchester rifle, a skinning knife, and a coffee pot containing live coals. When Packer arrived at the agency, he was haggard and worn but otherwise fit. Another of the original gold seekers asked where Packer had gotten his spending money. Packer admitted that the small band had starved in the San Juans, that Israel Swan had died from hunger and exposure, and that Packer had eaten him.

Two of the five men who had taken the Gunnison River route eventually arrived at the Los Pinós Indian Agency, and the remaining three men, including Loutsenhizer, arrived a few days later. General Charles Adams told the men he had already met Alfred Packer and that Packer spoke of being deserted by his companions. All five men immediately discredited Packer’s account to Adams and his staff. Loutsenhizer said the Winchester rifle Packer carried belonged to an elderly man in the party, and that the pipe Packer left at the agency belonged to Shannon Bell.

The following August, John A. Randolph, an illustrator for Harper's Weekly, found the site of the incident. Randolph discovered all five bodies at the foot of Slumgullion Pass, two miles southeast of Lake City, Colorado, in a pine-shaded gulch. The bodies lay above the Lake Fork of the Gunnison River, now colloquially known as Dead Man's Gulch. The site matched the description of where Packer had originally claimed that only Bell was killed. The snow covering the bodies and campsite had melted during the preceding four months. Randolph sketched the scene and alerted authorities in nearby Lake City. Harper's Weekly covered the story two months later, in its October 17, 1874, edition, and included Randolph's illustration.

The local coroner and law enforcement went to the site with about 20 volunteers and found all five bodies in various states of decomposition after four months outdoors. First responders said it appeared that extreme violence had befallen the men. Frank Miller's head was missing from the campsite. Frank Miller's and Israel Swan's corpses had been heavily scavenged and were little more than scattered bones, and Israel's skull had a jagged chunk missing from it. George Noon and James Humphrey were largely flayed torsos of rotting viscera attached to skeletal legs, but their faces remained intact and bearded. Humphrey's face was slightly more decayed than Noon's. Noon and Humphrey had head blows that suggested a hatchet and had noticeable broken bones.

Shannon Bell had skeletal legs, arms cut to the bones, hands that were still fully skinned, and a torso that was almost wholly flayed. Bell's remaining corpse was a mass of viscera with a thick red beard and bushy hair still on his face. The relatively undamaged state of Bell's face suggested that he had been the last to die. The top of Bell's skull had been ripped open. The three men whose bodies were still intact or partly intact had flesh and muscle cut from choice meaty locations; no attempt had been made to consume bone marrow or any organs. Preston Nutter identified the bodies as the five missing men. A rifle broken in two was found close to the bodies, and because of the damage to the remains, the rifle was presumed to have been used to bludgeon one or more of the men.

Nothing was heard from the six prospectors for nearly three months. The men became lost and, without proper provisions or equipment, were cold and hungry. Of the six men, only Packer lived.

### Alfred Packer’s background `established`

Alfred Packer, also known as John Schwartze, Alferd Packer, the Colorado Cannibal, and, in local newspapers, the “Man-Eater,” was an American prospector and self-proclaimed wilderness guide known for cannibalism. One account describes Alferd Packer, “The Colorado Cannibal,” as one of the most notorious and most misunderstood tales in Colorado history. The sources do not settle the spelling of his first name: official court papers from the time spell it “Alfred,” while Packer signed his name “Alferd”; his grave is also marked with a veteran’s tombstone listing “Alfred.” The Colorado State Archives’ Alfred Packer Collection includes military grave registration, along with legal and historical records concerning him.

Packer was one of three children born to James Packer and Esther Packer, née Griner. By the early 1850s, his father had moved the family to LaGrange County, Indiana, where he worked as a cabinetmaker. Packer is said to have had a bitter relationship with his parents. In his late teens, he moved to Minnesota and worked as a shoemaker. He suffered from epilepsy from an early age and was prone to seizures. During the American Civil War, he served in the Union Army. One account says he enlisted on April 22, 1862, in Winona, Minnesota, in Company F of the 16th Infantry Regiment, and was honorably discharged eight months later because epilepsy triggered seizures. He enlisted a second time, on June 25, 1863, in the 8th Iowa Cavalry Regiment at Ottumwa, Iowa, and was honorably discharged again on April 22, 1864, after his epilepsy caused further seizures. The shorter biographical account instead says that he enlisted twice and that both terms lasted only a few months because of epilepsy and seizures; both accounts agree that he was honorably discharged both times.

Afterward, Packer traveled west and worked at numerous jobs over the next nine years. He worked as a hunter, wagon teamster, ranch hand, field worker, range hand, and as someone who drove horses and other draft animals across the West. He also took odd jobs for mining companies. He worked for a couple of months as a guide, but people later said he was ill-suited to the role and prone to losing his way. He had a habit of getting lost, arguing with clients, stealing from people, and lying. His seizures and overall attitude meant that he never kept a job for long. He worked mining-related jobs from mine to mine but never found prosperity in mining; he worked briefly as a miner in the Colorado Territory and then moved on to the Utah Territory. He also tried mining in Colorado, but kept losing jobs because of his bad attitude and because no one could trust him. People who knew him generally disliked and distrusted him because of his argumentative personality, near-pathological or scurrilous lying, and reputation for theft.

In the fall of 1873, a group of 20 prospectors set out toward southwestern Colorado and the San Juan country. The group was traveling to newly found goldfields near Breckenridge, Colorado; another account spells the destination “Brackenridge.” Six men, including Packer, ultimately continued into the San Juan Mountains during the winter, and two months later only one man emerged from the mountains. Rather than looking emaciated, as early explorers were often later found, he looked weirdly fed. The surviving man was Alfred Packer. A separate account says that the six men set off from Bingham Canyon, Utah, on November 20, 1873, while another says Packer was one of six prospectors who headed into the Colorado mountains in February 1874. These accounts therefore give different dates for the expedition’s departure.

Packer was not initially with the party. George Tracy said that the party encountered a 23-year-old man called Alfred Packer near Provo. The expedition account says the group met him south of Salt Lake City, about 25 miles into the journey. Packer asked where the men were going, learned that they were headed for the goldfields, and offered his services as a guide. He claimed he was both a prospector and a guide who knew the territory between Utah and Breckenridge, or the San Juan territory, well. Because he had no provisions, he offered $25 to join the band. The prospectors accepted him because they needed a guide and thought that someone who knew the route would help them. Party members later said that Packer may have overstated or even fabricated his familiarity with the area. In fact, he had no idea where he was going, although he had worked as a guide for a few months.

During the journey, Packer was reported to have left with only a Colt revolver and no rifle. This conflicts with the account of his later arrival at the agency, which describes him carrying a rifle. He was also reported to be greedy with rations, lazy, obstinate, and obsessed with how much money the party carried. He quarreled constantly with Frank Miller. Preston Nutter later called him a “whining fraud” at Packer’s first trial. His epileptic seizures made his presence in the group strenuous, and there were rumors that he had spent time in jail in Salt Lake City on a charge of murdering a hunting partner.

On April 16, 1874, according to the agency account, men at the Los Piños Indian Agency near Saguache, Colorado, were eating breakfast when Packer appeared alone through a snowstorm. He wore rags over his feet instead of shoes, carried a coffee pot on one side, and had a rifle on the other. General Charles Adams, who oversaw the agency, had Alfred given food, and Alfred ate so fast that he threw up. Over whiskey, Alfred said that the others with him had become too exhausted to continue, that he had snow blindness and frostbite, and that he had been left behind to fend for himself. He claimed that he survived for months on rabbits, roots, and rosebuds and escaped only because of his experience as a guide.

Packer gave more than one account of what had happened, and the sources report that he was known for cannibalism. The Colorado State Archives’ collection includes his confessions, while the scientific team that exhumed the victims did not settle the spelling of his first name. The sources preserve both the story Packer told about surviving on plants and small animals and the broader historical account identifying him as the Colorado Cannibal and associating him with the cannibalism of his companions.

The Washington report likewise describes Packer as a “scurrilous liar and thief by reputation.”

## 7. Disputed & Uncertain

### Packer's claim of self-defense against Shannon Bell `contested`

*Held by Gulliford; disputed by UPI Archives.*

**Case for Packer’s self-defense claim** — Gulliford’s account supports Packer’s claim that he shot Shannon Bell in self-defense. Preston said that the rifle Alfred sold had belonged to an old man in the group, while the pipe Alfred left behind belonged to Shannon Bell. Alfred reported that Shannon then shot the remaining men. After being recaptured, Packer said that Shannon killed James Humphrey, George Noon, and Frank Miller as they slept around the campfire while the group was trying to find the Indian Agency. Packer also said that, when he returned from searching for food, Shannon accosted him with a hatchet, and that he fired twice with a pistol, shooting Bell in self-defense after Shannon went mad and attacked him. Another body was identified as Shannon Bell, whose remains had been stripped to the bones except for the hands and feet. On this account, it appears likely that Packer killed another prospector in self-defense before he began gnawing on the corpses of men who had already died. The case rests substantially on statements attributed to Alfred and Packer, including Packer’s own account after his recapture, together with the identification of Bell’s remains; those statements cannot be independently questioned here. (SRC_1 · SRC_2)

**Case against Packer’s self-defense claim** — UPI Archives disputes Packer’s claim on the basis of the physical evidence and the coroner’s conclusion. Two of the men had been struck in the head with a blunt object, and Shannon Bell also appeared to have suffered a blunt-force head injury. The coroner concluded that the men had been killed by blows to the head while they were asleep, which conflicts with Packer’s account that Bell attacked him with a hatchet and was shot in an act of self-defense. In addition, the bones of all five victims showed numerous knife marks indicating “defleshing.” This evidence can be read as indicating that the victims were attacked while sleeping and later had their bodies cut apart, rather than that Packer killed Bell only during an immediate confrontation. The opposing case therefore relies on the reported injuries and the coroner’s assessment, although the sources provided do not establish every detail of how those injuries occurred. (SRC_1 · SRC_7)

### Prosecution assertions that game was abundant and cannibalism was by choice `contested`

*Held by Scary Interesting.*

**Case for the prosecution’s assertions** — The hunters claimed that cannibalism must have been by choice because food was available. The prosecution made the same argument, asserting that food was plentiful in the area and therefore that Packer’s cannibalism could not have been forced by scarcity. This case is also supported by the claim that Packer was known for cannibalism, which presents the act as an established behavior rather than an isolated response to hunger. (SRC_1 · SRC_3)

**Case against the prosecution’s assertions** — The evidence against the assertions is that Packer’s spending had been frivolous. That fact complicates the assumption that the circumstances can be explained simply by plentiful food and voluntary cannibalism, although the supplied evidence does not itself establish exactly how his spending bears on food availability or his motives. The prosecution’s claim therefore rests on the statements of the hunters and the prosecution, while the contrary case has only this limited evidence and remains open. (SRC_3)

### Packer insisted he resorted to cannibalism due to complete desperation in an empty wilderness `contested`

*Held by Town of Lake City and history.denverlibrary.org; disputed by Scary Interesting.*

**Case for the claim** — The Town of Lake City and history.denverlibrary.org present Packer as someone who resorted to cannibalism because he was driven to desperation in the wilderness. Packer was known for cannibalism and confessed to it during the winter of 1874. Although he pleaded not guilty when charged with murder, Starrs believed he was more likely to have murdered his companions for their belongings and then resorted to cannibalism out of necessity rather than having intended cannibalism from the outset. Afterward, Packer went on to Saguache. On this account, the murder charge and the disputed circumstances of the deaths do not disprove his claim that hunger and isolation ultimately forced him to eat the bodies. (SRC_10 · SRC_3 · SRC_5)

**Case against the claim** — Scary Interesting disputes Packer’s insistence that complete desperation in an empty wilderness forced him to resort to cannibalism. Hunters testified that food remained available even during a bad winter. Local hunters and officials described the winter of 1874 as very severe, but said that the San Juan camp area still had plenty of deer, antelope, and smaller game. If that testimony is accurate, Packer was not trapped in an empty wilderness without alternatives, and cannibalism cannot readily be explained as a necessity caused by total starvation. This case rests on the testimony of those hunters and local officials, whose account conflicts with Packer’s version of his circumstances. (SRC_1 · SRC_3)
